## Regulation & obligation

### 1. Italy fined Meta €805 million and gave it sixty days to ask for consent properly

- **Summary:** The **Garante**, Italy's data protection authority, fined Meta Platforms Ireland €805 million on 14 November. It is the largest fine the authority has imposed. The decision concerns a Facebook for Android feature called "Get suggestions from your gallery while browsing Facebook", available since 2023. The app asked for one permission covering the whole app, behind a generic notice about "enabling other functions". Photos, videos and their metadata were read from about 20 million Android phones in Italy without specific consent. The amount is about 0.47 per cent of Meta's global revenue. Meta must switch the feature off by default within 60 days and seek consent separately, and report its initiatives within 30 days and the measures adopted within 90. The investigation opened in July 2025 after a complaint from a consumer group in Bolzano. The Garante applied article 122 of Italy's privacy code, which transposes the ePrivacy directive, and rejected Meta's argument that Ireland's regulator should handle the case. Meta says it will appeal.
- **Why it matters:** The defect is the single permission, not the feature. One app-wide grant covering several functions is what many mobile applications still ask for, and the Garante has now put a price on it. For Australian and New Zealand teams the parallel is domestic. The Privacy Act requires notice of what is collected and why, at the point of collection, and an AI feature that is on by default and explained in a general settings screen fails that test on this reasoning.
- **Source:** [Garante per la protezione dei dati personali — Facebook: il Garante privacy sanziona Meta per 805 milioni di euro](https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10321080) &middot; [BBC News — Italy fines Meta €805m over accessing users' photos](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cwn83gxemk35o)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** Mandated

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. The US Treasury told Nvidia's board to hire an outsider and answer within ten days

- **Summary:** **Scott Bessent**, the US Treasury Secretary, said on Fox News on 15 November that Nvidia's board should look outside the company for its next chief executive. "This makes me think that as the board conducts a search for a new leader, that they need to go outside the company," he said. He demanded answers within ten days from the board and its outside counsel. The questions concern whether Nvidia knowingly pursued what he called "seemingly illicit" chip sales to China through shell companies, many of them in Singapore. He compared the case to a bank that had failed its compliance obligations and would have had executives removed. Bessent separately rejected calls to write tighter export controls into law, saying "we have all the rules we need". Congress is working on a bipartisan export bill that could pass within weeks. Nvidia has hired Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to lead its own investigation and reports quarterly results on Tuesday.
- **Why it matters:** The argument has moved from export rules to corporate governance. A cabinet secretary is asking whether a compliance function failed and at what level, which is the test banks face after a sanctions breach. That framing travels: an Australian or New Zealand buyer of GPU capacity is now assessing a supplier with an interim chief executive and an open compliance question. The ten days is a political deadline rather than a legal one, and the board can miss it without consequence.

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. A US judge let a memory-price conspiracy case proceed, with the AI buildout as its subject

- **Summary:** Judge **Noël Wise** of the Northern District of California refused on 14 November to dismiss a class action against Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron. The suit was filed on 25 June 2026 by 17 consumers and small businesses. It alleges the three companies, which held more than 91 per cent of global DRAM revenue in the third quarter of 2025, cut conventional memory production from late 2022. They then moved wafer capacity to high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators, which needs three to four times the wafer area per gigabyte. Conventional DRAM prices rose 171.8 per cent in a year by that quarter, and the complaint alleges 697 per cent over 18 months. None of the three expanded capacity. The companies argued the case repeated a 2016 to 2018 action that the Ninth Circuit dismissed. Judge Wise disagreed, finding the allegations "more akin to hawks flying in formation". Answers are due on 7 December.
- **Why it matters:** The constraint on AI compute is now a legal question as well as a commercial one. If the case reaches discovery, the high-bandwidth memory contracts behind every accelerator order become evidence. For Australian and New Zealand buyers the effect is timing, because a ruling that returned capacity to commodity memory would move prices already built into data-centre business cases. The allegations are untested, and a denied motion to dismiss decides nothing about the merits.
- **Source:** [LitNews — Judge refuses to dismiss DRAM supply conspiracy suit against Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron](https://litnews.ai/articles/garciaguirre-samsung-sk-hynix-micron-dram-conspiracy-suit-survives-dismissal) &middot; [Wccftech — Federal judge rejects memory makers' defense to advance class-action antitrust lawsuit](https://wccftech.com/federal-judge-rejects-memory-makers-defense-samsung-micron-sk-hynix-dram-shortage-lawsuit/)

## Research & evidence

### 1. Stanford published code for a memory system that hands a frozen model sixteen tokens

- **Summary:** The **SNAP group** at Stanford released Memorilla on 14 November, with code and a training recipe under Apache 2.0. The system reads a document collection once and stores it as reusable embeddings. For each question it learns to place what matters into a fixed set of 16 memory tokens in the model's input, rather than pasting documents into the context window. It runs against a frozen Qwen3-8B, so the base model is never retrained. The paper, posted to arXiv on 15 October and revised on 3 November, reports the best score on five of eight benchmarks against Mem0 and MemoRAG. On FactKG it reports 84.33 per cent accuracy against 80.43 per cent for full-context inference, using 108 times fewer tokens. In a TextWorld agent test it lifts success from 18.6 to 24.2 per cent.
- **Why it matters:** The claim worth testing is that 16 tokens can beat the full document set. That cuts against the assumption that longer context is the answer to memory, and it points at where agents lose track over long runs. The numbers are the authors' own, on their own benchmarks, and nobody else has replicated them. For an organisation weighing retrieval against long context, the useful step is the same either way: run the comparison on your own corpus before moving a production system.
- **Source:** [GitHub — snap-stanford/memorilla](https://github.com/snap-stanford/memorilla) &middot; [arXiv — Memorilla: Latent Semantic Memory for LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21375)
- **Impact:** Low
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Capability:** Incremental
- **Evidence:** Unreplicated preprint
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (an academic preprint with public code, benchmark data and a named author list; the results are the authors' measurements and have not been independently reproduced)
- **Verification:** Verified
