# Executive Summary

**Top stories.** The most consequential AI story of the window is the first evidenced, priced example of autonomous agents running a criminal intrusion campaign at scale. **NorForge** principal threat researcher **Snorre Fagerland** found more than **20 exposed Cairn panels** — Cairn is an open-source, state-based AI problem-solving engine — in active malicious use from around **2 October 2026**, the busiest cluster (**AIA0001**, 14 panels, **1,100-plus attack projects**) using instructions of fewer than **100 Chinese characters** to hunt unauthenticated remote code execution on real e-commerce sites across the **United States, Canada, Japan and Australia**. By late last week the agents had **attempted more than 1,200 sites and breached over 100**, at an estimated **23 hours** and about **US$50 in tokens per success**, with **Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins** named as victims by Reuters and an **Australian e-commerce case** documented with confirmed command execution. Second, Australia's **Fair Work Commission** began enforcing generative-AI obligations on **20 October**: anyone filing AI-assisted documents must **disclose** the use, have a **person** — not another AI tool — verify every fact, case and quote, and confirm witness statements reflect the witness's own words, with non-compliance risking costs orders, disregarded documents or dismissal, and false information carrying up to **12 months' imprisonment**.

Two things follow. First, **the agentic-risk scenario has moved from hypothetical to accounted for**: the compelling numbers in the Cairn case are not capability scores but price and throughput — roughly US$5,000 of tokens for a campaign that touched 1,200 sites — and the discipline that breaks the economics is the ordinary one of detecting automated probing before it succeeds. Second, **the obligation layer moved in Australia and contracted everywhere else**: the Fair Work Commission converted AI-slop risk into a binding procedural duty with a commencement date, while **Meta** proposed to shift liability for its own AI's mistakes onto enterprise customers from **30 October** and **OpenAI** stood up a voluntary Australian taskforce on incident notification — a duty created, a duty disclaimed, and a duty volunteered, in the same 48 hours.

## Regulation & obligation

### 1. The Fair Work Commission's generative-AI duties took effect on 20 October

- **Summary:** The Fair Work Commission's *Guidance note: Use of generative AI in Commission cases* took effect on **20 October 2026**, attaching three requirements to anyone using generative AI to prepare documents filed in Commission matters: **disclose** that generative AI was used and how; **verify** — by a person, not by another AI tool — that every fact, legislative reference, case and quotation is accurate and real; and, for witness statements and declarations, **confirm** that the content reflects the witness's own knowledge and words. Non-compliance can see documents disregarded, costs orders made against the party, or the case dismissed; knowingly providing false or misleading information carries up to **12 months' imprisonment** under **Criminal Code s 137.1**. The Commission's own research found roughly **40 per cent** of surveyed applicants had used AI to prepare or manage a case, and lodgements rose **44 per cent** between 2022–23 and 2024–25. **Why it matters:** this is an Australian regulator converting generative-AI-slop risk into a binding procedural obligation with an operative commencement date inside the window — the clearest AUNZ duty-layer movement of the period, and the first place a costs order for an unverified AI citation can be expected. The obligation commenced **20 October**; the trade-press report of it carries a **21 October** dateline, and the instrument itself is the primary source.
- **Source:** [Fair Work Commission — Guidance note: Use of generative AI in Commission cases](https://www.fwc.gov.au/documents/resources/guidance-note-use-of-generative-ai-in-commission-cases.pdf)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** Commenced

### 2. A former AI Expert Group member told a parliamentary inquiry Australia's agent-liability gap needs urgent reform

The evidence lands as the committee's **30 November 2026** reporting deadline approaches, and follows a period in which the practical question of agent accountability has been argued in Australian forums rather than legislated. **Why it matters:** the liability question is the operational form of the obligation debate — disclosure and verification duties tell a practitioner what to do, but nothing in Australian law yet says who answers when an autonomous agent acts wrongly on a client's behalf, and the inquiry is the mechanism through which that could change. ### 3. Meta's proposed commercial terms shift liability for its AI's mistakes onto enterprise customers from 30 October

- **Summary:** **Meta's** proposed commercial-terms update, scheduled to take effect **30 October 2026**, states that AI features "may make mistakes" and that customers "are solely responsible for and assume any and all risks associated with such AI features and any resulting content" — moving liability for model error from the vendor to the enterprise customer. The same update claims a licence to crawl and index content "associated with your products or services", which consultants read as ending any practical reliance on **robots.txt**; Meta subsequently told **CIO.com** it was reworking that wording because it applies only to ads and business products. Named analysts — **Frank Dickson**, **Mike Wilkes**, **Thomas Randall** and **Aman Mahapatra** — describe the liability clause as inverting accountability and the crawling licence as open-ended. **Why it matters:** for AUNZ enterprises this is a contractual risk transfer rather than a policy proposal, and it arrives in the same window as live questions about who answers for an agent's errors: the asymmetry to note is that a customer accepts the risk of a vendor's model while retaining none of the vendor's ability to test or monitor it. The terms are draft, and Meta says it will revise them.
- **Source:** [Computerworld — Meta's new commercial terms say if its AI makes a mistake it will legally be the customer's fault](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4237414/metas-new-commercial-terms-say-if-its-ai-makes-a-mistake-it-will-legally-be-the-customers-fault.html)

### 1. METR reviewed Anthropic's misalignment risk assessment — and found the methods weaker than the conclusion

- **Summary:** **METR** published an external review on **20 October** of the "Misalignment in high-stakes settings" section of **Anthropic's Risk Report: August 2026**, which concludes the risk of catastrophic harm from misalignment of its models is "**low**" as of **15 July 2026**. METR **agrees** the risk is low for the covered models, but **primarily because the models' capabilities are limited — not because safeguards and alignment training work** — and warns the report's covert-capability benchmarks are a poor proxy for evading Anthropic's real monitoring system. It notes recent incidents "undermine several of the report's arguments", and recommends publishing four redacted items, end-to-end red-teaming of the monitoring, and adding a risk pathway for AIs that undermine safety-relevant decisions at Anthropic. As part of the process Anthropic had the right to veto or redact, but **no redactions were made** and METR did not change its conclusions. **Why it matters:** this is the clearest example in the window of an independent evaluator testing a frontier lab's own safety case rather than its model, and it is precisely the "evidence layer" role the source spine was built around — a third-party review with documentary access that reaches the same headline verdict for different and narrower reasons.

### 2. IBM says prompt-level memory put a stock GPT-4.1 agent top of a customer-service leaderboard

- **Summary:** **IBM Research's** **ALTK-Evolve** distils an agent's own training-run successes and failures into short behavioural guidelines appended to the system prompt, then applies them unchanged on held-out tasks — no weight updates and no human annotation. Using stock **GPT-4.1** it reaches **Pass^4 = 67.5 per cent** on **τ²-bench retail**, above every frontier entry and ahead of **RAFT-30B-A3B** (**61.4 per cent**), a model fine-tuned on synthetic τ²-bench data; with **GPT-5.5** it reaches **82.0 per cent** on the airline domain, clearing all frontier entries (66–72 per cent). Reliability (**Pass^4**) rose about **10 points** in both domains while conversations got slightly shorter. **Why it matters:** it is a cheap, reproducible demonstration that agent reliability can be bought with memory and prompt engineering rather than a larger model, which is directly relevant to AUNZ enterprises costing agentic deployments — but every figure is **IBM's own** and the benchmark is a single-company public leaderboard, so treat the ranking as a vendor claim until an independent run reproduces it.
- **Source:** [Hugging Face — IBM Research: ALTK-Evolve](https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-research/altk-evolve-bc)
- **Impact:** Low
- **Evidence:** Vendor claim
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (vendor, self-reported) (IBM's own write-up and public leaderboard submissions; not independently replicated)
- **Verification:** Reported
