## Regulation & obligation

### 1. The global music industry told Albanese that an AI copyright carve-out would be a "disaster"

- **Summary:** The **International Federation of the Phonographic Industry** has warned Australia against weakening copyright law to let AI companies train on local material. Its chief executive, Victoria Oakley, told Guardian Australia that a carve-out would be "ridiculous and dangerous". She argued that a single safe harbour would let a model train on music from anywhere in the world. The intervention lands while the government weighs changes AI companies have requested. Anthropic has proposed an opt-out model, where content is scraped unless a rights holder objects. OpenAI told a parliamentary committee that licensing music for streaming is a different business from training on it. The Attorney-General, Michelle Rowland, has said any changes must deliver "meaningful control and fair compensation". Australian labels have raised constitutional concerns about a compensation case.
- **Why it matters:** A safe harbour granted here would cover training that reaches every market, so the cost falls on rights holders who never agreed to it. That is why the lobby is offshore: Australia is being argued about as a precedent, not as a market. The Joint Select Committee on AI reports by 30 November, and copyright is the question it cannot avoid. Read the committee's findings before the minister's next statement, because the committee is where the text will first appear.
- **Source:** [The Guardian — Weakening AI copyright law will spell 'disaster for creativity', global music body warns Albanese](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/09/weakening-ai-copyright-law-will-spell-disaster-for-creativity-australia)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** Signalled
- **Obligation ref:** Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) — the fair-dealing exceptions in ss 40–43 and the flexible exception in s 200AB; AI companies have asked for a training exception, and no bill has been introduced

### 2. Elvis Presley's rights holders and Eminem's publisher sued Suno under Tennessee's voice law

- **Summary:** **Two groups of music rights holders** filed federal lawsuits against the AI music generator Suno in the Northern District of California. The Presley action, brought by Authentic Brands Group, Raleigh Music Publishing and others, runs to 51 pages. It alleges copyright infringement and a breach of Tennessee's Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security Act, a 2024 law against cloning a singer's voice. The filing says Suno reproduces Presley's "rich vocal timbre" without permission. Eight Mile Style, which controls much of Eminem's catalogue, filed separately over generated songs and lyrics; Eminem is not a party. Suno said it blocks prompts naming specific artists and screens uploads.
- **Why it matters:** Australia has no likeness right for a voice. A local artist's remedy runs through passing off and section 18 of the Australian Consumer Law, both of which need a misleading representation rather than a cloned timbre. The United States is where the rule is being written, state by state, and the ELVIS Act is the first statute to name the harm. AUNZ labels and studios should assume the licensing market forms offshore first.
- **Source:** [Variety — Elvis Music Owners, Eminem Publisher File Sprawling New Lawsuits Against AI Music Generator Suno](https://variety.com/2026/music/news/elvis-eminem-sue-suno-ai-music-generator-1236941798/)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (specialist trade coverage quoting the filed complaints and Suno's response; the allegations are untested and Suno denies them)

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. Congress turned on Nvidia's China business after a spy report and an empty office

- **Summary:** **Three House Republican committee chairs** asked Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, to appear for a closed-door briefing on 18 November, a day after the company reports quarterly earnings. The letter cites national-security concerns about Chinese access to sensitive technology. Senator Chris Coons, in line to chair Foreign Relations in January, said Congress would investigate. The reports behind it: The New York Times said a memo identified Megaspeed executive Alice Huang as recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. Nvidia says Huang's contacts with her were ordinary customer meetings. Separately, Senator John Cornyn publicised a Times report that Commerce approved New Matrix to assemble Nvidia chips in Beijing despite little evidence the firm operates. Its owners are linked to a blacklisted private equity firm.
- **Why it matters:** Export control is now enforced by headline as well as by licence, and that raises the political risk attached to every allocation of Nvidia capacity. AUNZ buyers, including sovereign AI programmes and research clouds, compete for the same supply. The second finding is the more useful one for them: an approval on paper is not evidence of a counterparty, and due diligence on a broker is now part of procurement.
- **Source:** [CNBC — Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang faces bipartisan congressional scrutiny over reported China spy link](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/08/jensen-huang-nvidia-china-spy-congress.html) &middot; [The New York Times — US approved a Chinese firm with ties to blacklisted company to buy Nvidia chips](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/07/business/economy/nvidia-chips-chinese-firms.html)

### 2. Anthropic will list at a cut valuation into the backlash the midterms produced

Its amended prospectus discloses about $895.4 billion of purchase commitments and leases, substantially all for compute and infrastructure. The Nasdaq had its steepest one-day fall in 19 months the previous Thursday. - **Why it matters:** This is the first time a pure-play AI company has had to price political opposition into an equity story. ## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. South-east Asia's datacentre boom hit a power and water wall

- **Summary:** **Thailand** paused construction on 49 datacentre projects and approval decisions on 117 more in September, over insufficient detail for regulation and monitoring. The governor of West Java halted a project for want of permits and an incomplete environmental assessment. The pause follows a year of extraordinary growth: operational capacity in Malaysia's Johor rose from 94 megawatts in 2023 to 1,110 megawatts in 2026, with 3,088 megawatts more planned. Wood Mackenzie estimates Johor's datacentres already use 24 per cent of the state's power, rising to 40 per cent by 2035. Australian operator AirTrunk plans to spend $3 billion doubling its Johor sites to four.
- **Why it matters:** This is the sequence Australia is entering, compressed into two years. Capacity arrives before the grid connection and the water allocation, and the objections follow the construction. The NSW upper-house inquiry reported on 5 November that its planning framework assesses each project alone, while the cumulative effects land on one substation. AUNZ operators should read a signed power purchase agreement as the real approval, and a planning consent as the weaker one.
- **Source:** [The Guardian — The datacentre arms race hits pause in south-east Asia](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/07/datacentres-southeast-asia)
