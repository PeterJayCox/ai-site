## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. A model marketplace pulled three providers over logged prompts

- **Summary:** OpenRouter off-boarded three inference providers and removed 125 endpoints from its Zero Data Retention eligibility list. The marketplace announced the action on 22 November, after Dragos researchers ran canary-token prompts against hosted models from July to November. Their tests found resellers logging prompts, and providers ignoring no-collection flags. OpenRouter says no breach touched its own systems. Three providers have posted notices: Together, which admitted prompt leakage for requests made in June and July; Novita, on MiniMax requests; and Phala, in a preliminary incident report.
- **Why it matters:** A retention badge on a model marketplace is a promise, and nothing was auditing it. Dragos only found this by planting tokens and watching where they surfaced. Any team sending proprietary code or customer data through a reseller should treat the label as unverified, and check which provider actually serves its endpoint. Australian and New Zealand firms that buy inference through aggregators rather than direct now have a documented failure mode in that chain.
- **Source:** [OpenRouter — Data Processing Incidents on Inference Providers](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/data-processing-incidents-on-inference-providers/)

- **Evidence:** Corroborated

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. The world agreed to keep talking about autonomous weapons until 2028

- **Summary:** The UN talks on lethal autonomous weapons ended in Geneva on 21 November with an agreement to elaborate a possible instrument by 2028. All 128 states party to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons backed the outcome, reached after an all-night session. Around 100 states wanted a mandate for binding rules. The United States, Russia and Israel opposed one, and the text leaves the question of legal force open. The Swiss chair, Julien Thoeni, called it a significant humanitarian achievement. Stop Killer Robots said the delay puts civilians at risk.
- **Why it matters:** A consensus body of 128 states moves at the pace of its least willing member, and three large arms producers are the least willing. Weapons that select and engage targets are already in use in Ukraine, Sudan and the Middle East. So the practical effect of this outcome is to leave targeting decisions to national policy for at least two more years. Defence suppliers and dual-use exporters should read those national rules as the binding constraint, not the UN process.
- **Source:** [Reuters — States agree to UN talks aiming to regulate 'killer robots' by 2028](https://www.reuters.com/world/states-agree-to-un-talks-aiming-regulate-killer-robots-by-2028-2026-11-21/)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** Signalled
- **Obligation ref:** No instrument. CCW Seventh Review Conference outcome, Geneva, 21 November 2026 — an agreement to elaborate a possible instrument by 2028, with no text, no negotiating mandate and no commencement.
- **Evidence:** Confirmed

## Regulation & obligation

### 1. Four governors asked Congress to write the frontier-model rules

- **Summary:** The National Governors Association published AI principles on 22 November, after work by a bipartisan council. It was led by Maryland's Wes Moore, the association's chair, and Indiana's Mike Braun, its vice chair, with North Carolina's Josh Stein and Utah's Spencer Cox. The document asks Congress to set complementary federal and state roles. Washington would take national and international risks, with independent evaluation of the most advanced models, and incident reports to federal authorities and affected states. States would keep their powers over consumers, discrimination and workforce transition.
- **Why it matters:** The argument is over where the line between federal and state AI law sits, and this plants a marker from both parties. It also leaves the hardest definition open: the principles set no compute or revenue threshold for what counts as a frontier model. Labs would get one set of rules to track. Companies building on top of their models keep facing state law, which is a different settlement from the pre-emption the industry has lobbied for.
- **Source:** [National Governors Association — Governors Call for AI Action](https://www.nga.org/news/press-releases/governors-call-for-ai-action/)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** Proposed
- **Obligation ref:** No instrument. NGA Governors Council to Secure American AI Dominance, "Federal and State Roles in AI Regulation: Statement of Principles", 22 November 2026 — principles addressed to Congress and the states, with no bill, no operative provision and no commencement.
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (the association's own release and principles document)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-22

### 2. New Zealand is reviewing copyright for AI-assisted works

- **Summary:** RNZ reported on 23 November that New Zealand's Government is reviewing copyright legislation, and that an Auckland scholar wants clearer rules for works made with AI. Joshua Yuvaraj, of Auckland Law School, argues that protection should turn on genuine human contribution. He grounds that in emotion, morality and community, and says a work made with AI assistance can still qualify where a person supplies real skill and labour, including through prompts. He also wants the law to be clear on training data and on compensation for artists.
- **Why it matters:** New Zealand has no settled test for AI-assisted authorship, and a review with no bill in front of it is the cheapest point to influence. The practical advice in the piece is already useful: document what the human contributed. New Zealand is in a caretaker period after the 7 November election, so expect nothing to be enacted until a government forms. Australian creators face the same question under a different statute, and the two regimes have diverged before.
- **Source:** [RNZ — Should AI-assisted creative works be eligible for copyright protection?](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/business/2740626/should-ai-assisted-creative-works-be-eligible-for-copyright-protection)

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. OpenAI published its own loss curve and proposed an industry limit

- **Summary:** OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, published an argument on 22 November for treating perplexity as the industry's shared measure of progress. The post plots log-perplexity falling from about 0.99 at GPT-3.5 to below 0.8 in current models. It links that drop to capabilities appearing in mathematics and cybersecurity. He proposes that labs commit to shared limits, measured on defined metrics, and not cross them until monitoring has caught up. He concedes that labs use different datasets, so the numbers do not compare directly.
- **Why it matters:** Every figure here is OpenAI's own, measured on an internal dataset it does not publish, so the curve is a claim rather than a measurement anyone can check. The proposal matters more than the plot. A limit needs a metric all labs accept, an agreed threshold and a way to verify compliance, and this post supplies only the first. It is an opening bid in a standards argument, and it argues for pacing on terms the vendor has chosen.
- **Source:** [OpenAI — Perplexity and Intelligence](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/)
