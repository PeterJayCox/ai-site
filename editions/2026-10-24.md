# Executive Summary

**Top stories.** The window's dominant story is the one the last two editions could only preview, and it has moved from disclosure to consequence. The two critical **PHP** flaws that frontier models found and that were patched on **21 October** are now being attacked at internet scale: security vendors counted **millions of exploitation attempts** within 24 hours of the identifiers leaking, and by **23 October** the global response had acquired its own name — **"Black October"** — with **Figma's** senior director of product management, **John Milinovich**, writing that "in the last 2 days there have been several dozen cyber attacks which, in isolation, would each have been the worst to happen in an entire calendar year." Former **CISA** director **Chris Krebs** used the moment to tell smaller organisations to scan "constantly", patch, or take legacy systems offline, warning the problem will get "worse before it gets better"; **Anthropic's** deputy chief information security officer said the same. Second, **an established European party ran AI deepfakes of named individuals on its official accounts and dared the regulator to respond**: Spain's opposition **People's Party** published a video, launched **this week** on its own social channels, using AI to make the prime minister's wife **Begoña Gómez** and former Socialist president **José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero** appear to admit to wrongdoing, drawing nearly **2 million** views and a public rebuke from Spain's digital transformation minister — and landing squarely inside the **EU AI Act's** deepfake-disclosure duty, in force since **2 August 2026**. Third, **Google** answered the energy objection to its own build-out with money rather than an argument: a **1-gigawatt virtual power plant** with **Voltus** across the **PJM** grid, under which businesses and households are paid to cut consumption at peak.

Three things follow. First, **the dual-use ledger has now been settled empirically in the offensive direction**: the same capability that found PHP's flaws "before attackers did" has produced a scramble that governments and banks are still inside, which means the doctrine question for AUNZ readers is no longer whether AI-driven discovery changes the economics of vulnerabilities — it demonstrably has — but whether their own patch cadence assumes the old timeline. Second, **the deepfake question has moved from a hypothetical to a party's campaign budget**: the Spanish ad is precisely the case the AI Act's labelling rule was written for, which turns a compliance question for every AUNZ platform, agency and broadcaster into a live one about who enforces, and how fast, when a political actor breaks it deliberately. Third, **the AI build-out is now paying for its own social licence in cash, not promises** — Google buying demand response, and the hyperscalers retreating from secrecy clauses — which is the industry-side version of the standards Australia is legislating for large data centres.

## Regulation & obligation

### 1. Spain's opposition party ran AI deepfakes of political figures on its own accounts, days into an election campaign

- **Summary:** Spain's conservative **People's Party (PP)** launched a video on its official social channels **this week** that used artificial intelligence to manipulate the likenesses of people connected to Prime Minister **Pedro Sánchez's** Socialist party, making them appear to admit to wrongdoing. An AI-generated version of Sánchez's wife, **Begoña Gómez**, speaks about receiving a university post thanks to her husband's job, and a deepfake of former Socialist president **José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero** speaks about hiding jewellery — both nods to live investigations in which Gómez and Zapatero deny wrongdoing. By **23 October** the video had close to **2 million** views, and Spain's digital transformation minister, **Óscar López**, told the broadcaster **TVE** that it was "telling people a colossal lie, and for a party to do it on its official accounts seems intolerable to me". The intervention lands with a general election due next month and directly engages **Article 50(4) of the EU AI Act**, which has required deployers to disclose that image, audio or video content is a deepfake since **2 August 2026**. **Why it matters:** it is the first test of Europe's deepfake-disclosure duty in a national campaign, and the test is a political party rather than a vendor — the actor with the least incentive to comply and the most to gain from not doing so. For AUNZ readers the read is procedural: the obligation exists, the disclosure plainly was not made, and the question is now one of enforcement speed, which is precisely the gap the Australian joint committee's proposals on incident reporting and independent evaluators are trying to close. This is a masthead report of a public video and the official response to it; no regulator has yet opened a proceeding, and the PP's position is not represented in the coverage.

### 2. OpenAI signed a safety-research partnership with Poland's national AI institute, extending the AISI network

- **Summary:** **OpenAI** announced on **23 October** that it has signed an agreement with **NASK**, Poland's National Research Institute, to collaborate on AI safety, security and technical research. The counterpart is NASK's **AI Safety Research Center**, which serves as the **Polish Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute**; the work covers evaluating increasingly capable systems, identifying vulnerabilities and strengthening safeguards, with NASK also pursuing detection of synthetic AI-generated content and the multilingual dimension of that problem. NASK's director, **Radosław Nielek**, framed it as a public-institute-to-developer arrangement — "AI safety cannot be achieved in isolation" — and OpenAI's VP and head of EMEA policy, **Tom Duff Gordon**, cited Poland's scientific base, noting that many of OpenAI's own researchers are Polish. OpenAI lists its equivalent arrangements with the **US Center for Advancing Standards and Innovation for Super Intelligence (CAISSI)**, the **UK AI Safety Institute** and other public research institutions as precursors. **Why it matters:** it is the network Australia's own **Australian AI Safety Institute** is being built into — the AISI has an MOU with the **UK AISI** — and it shows the evaluation layer internationalising faster than the regulatory layer, which is the practical answer to the question an AUNZ reader should be asking about who actually tests a frontier model before it is deployed here. The announcement and the framing are **OpenAI's own**; no terms, funding or joint publication has been disclosed.

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. The AI-found PHP flaws are being exploited at internet scale, and the response has a name: "Black October"

- **Summary:** The two critical **PHP** flaws found with frontier models — **CVE-2026-109618** and **CVE-2026-109619**, both **CVSS 9.2**, patched on **21 October** — moved from disclosure to mass exploitation inside 48 hours. Security vendors told **The Guardian** on **22 October** that they had logged **millions of attempts** to exploit the flaw since the identifiers leaked, and **CNBC** reported on **23 October** that cybersecurity teams worldwide were still rushing to patch, in a moment the industry has labelled **"Black October"**. **Figma's** senior director of product management, **John Milinovich**, wrote that "in the last 2 days there have been several dozen cyber attacks which, in isolation, would each have been the worst to happen in an entire calendar year. Every sector from government to education to big tech and banks is being massively affected." Former **CISA** director **Chris Krebs** told smaller companies to "constantly" scan for vulnerabilities, deploy patches or take legacy systems offline, and said the situation would get "worse before it gets better"; **Anthropic's** deputy CISO, **Jason Clinton**, said the cybersecurity landscape is likely to get "a lot worse". Roughly **70 per cent** of websites run PHP. **Why it matters:** this is the consequence half of the dual-use story this product carried on 22 October — a flaw that frontier models were credited with finding "before attackers did" has nonetheless produced an internet-scale scramble, which says the defensive value of AI-assisted discovery is only realised if patch cadence keeps up. It resets urgency for every AUNZ organisation running PHP, from government service portals to e-commerce, and it is the sharpest available evidence for the position that AI compresses the interval between disclosure and exploitation. The exploitation counts come from commercial security vendors and the escalation framing is the industry's own; the CNBC report is the trade press's account rather than a regulator's.

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. Google and Voltus will build a 1-gigawatt virtual power plant so Google's data centres can draw more

- **Summary:** **Google** announced on **22 October** an expanded agreement with the energy supplier **Voltus** to build a **1-gigawatt virtual power plant** across the **PJM** grid region, which serves **67 million** people across 13 states and Washington DC. The plant generates nothing itself: Voltus aggregates flexible capacity from businesses and households — smart thermostats, batteries, industrial assets — and during peak demand those assets temporarily reduce consumption to support grid reliability, with participants paid through bill savings and cash. The deal is a **ten-fold** expansion of a **100-megawatt** agreement the two signed earlier this year, and Google describes it as one of the largest known distributed-capacity portfolios deployed by a hyperscaler. **Why it matters:** it is the industry-side answer to the objection that is now the binding constraint on AI build-out in AUNZ as much as in the US — that data centres raise everyone else's power bills — and Google is paying for demand reduction rather than arguing about it. It sits directly beside Australia's proposed mandatory standards, under which large data centres would be obliged to underwrite their own new supply, pay their full share of connection costs and reduce power when needed; Google's is voluntary and its own, but it is the same lever. The **1 GW** figure and the portfolio description are **Google's own**; no independent measure of delivered capacity has been published.

### 2. Hyperscalers began dismantling the secrecy clauses that have driven data-centre opposition

- **Summary:** The hyperscalers are moving to de-escalate the disputes their own construction secrecy has produced. **Amazon** published a **Data Center Commitment** earlier this month under which it would disclose more about energy and water use and consider a moratorium on asking officials to sign non-disclosure agreements covering planning applications; **Meta** said this week that it is reviewing its use of NDAs after sustained criticism of the secrecy around **Hyperion**, its Louisiana project; **Microsoft** committed in **March** to cease using NDAs in site selection and development, and **Google** has since followed. **Computerworld** reported the trend on **23 October**. **Why it matters:** the NDA was the mechanism that turned a planning process into a grievance, and its withdrawal is the industry conceding that community consent is now a prerequisite rather than an obstacle — the same social-licence argument the UK **SAIL** taskforce put to ministers on 22 October, and the objection Australia's own tiered large-data-centre standards are being written to price in. Each commitment is the company's own; none has been independently audited, and Amazon's moratorium is framed as a consideration rather than a rule.

The precise headcount is reported at between roughly **3,500** and **3,850** depending on the outlet.
