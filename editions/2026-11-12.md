## Regulation & obligation

### 1. England's universities regulator told institutions to bring back exams

- **Summary:** The **Office for Students** published research with Advance HE on 11 November, drawing on interviews with more than 300 students and staff at over 120 institutions. It found that unsupervised assessment can now be completed to a high standard with AI, and that detection tools do not reliably identify AI use. Students described peers who could not recall details of work they had just submitted. The regulator's director of strategy called AI a generational risk to the system. Institutions that fail to design valid and reliable measures of knowledge could breach their conditions of registration and face sanctions, the regulator said. It pointed to closed-book exams, presentations and oral vivas.
- **Why it matters:** The lever is registration, which is the licence to award degrees and to recruit international students. That is why a research report carries a sanction threat attached. The finding underneath it is that staff cannot separate AI-assisted work from unaided work, so the assessment method itself becomes the compliance risk. Australian and New Zealand providers running joint awards or recruiting through UK pathways should expect agents and partners to ask which of their assessments are supervised, and to want the answer in writing.
- **Source:** [Office for Students — Making AI work for students: reflections on institutional practice](https://www.officeforstudents.org.uk/publications/making-ai-work-for-students-reflections-on-institutional-practice/) &middot; [The Guardian — AI has made it 'close to impossible' to assess university coursework, regulator says](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2026/nov/11/ai-close-to-impossible-assess-university-coursework-regulator)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** Commenced
- **Obligation ref:** Higher Education and Research Act 2017 (UK) — the Office for Students' ongoing conditions of registration in its regulatory framework, breach of which can lead to sanctions; restated for AI in OfS 2026.56, 11 November 2026
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (the regulator's own research report, OfS 2026.56, dated 11 November 2026; its findings are self-reported by students and staff, and the sanction threat is the regulator's own statement)
- **Verification:** Verified

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. DeepMind says its agents formally verified Signal's protocol in four weeks

- **Summary:** **Google DeepMind** published AlphaProof Code on 10 November, a multi-model agent framework that writes formal proofs in Lean. Researchers used it to prove security properties of the compiled Signal protocol, not just its design. The company says the agents generated more than six million lines of Lean and that human experts reviewed about one per cent of the codebase. It says the work took four weeks, against years for expert teams historically. To test the framework, the researchers reintroduced two previously fixed bugs; the system proved both versions insecure. The verification artifacts are open source and a technical paper is published. Signal's chief technology officer called it a major breakthrough.
- **Why it matters:** Proofs that reach compiled code, rather than the design above it, are the assurance level critical-infrastructure rules assume and rarely receive. The claim is a vendor's, but the proofs are machine-checked and the artifacts are public, which makes it checkable in a way most capability claims are not. Read the paper before the post: the human review covered one per cent, and the proofs establish the properties the researchers specified. They do not establish that no other flaw exists.
- **Source:** [Google DeepMind — Introducing AlphaProof Code: a breakthrough in verifying complex software at depth](https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Capability:** Frontier
- **Evidence:** Vendor claim
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (vendor, self-reported: DeepMind built the framework, ran the verification and characterises the result; the Lean artifacts and paper are published for independent checking, and Signal's CTO is quoted by the vendor)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-10

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. Datacentre developers plan 181 gigawatts of on-site gas to jump the grid queue

- **Summary:** **BloombergNEF** published research on 11 November counting 181 gigawatts of gas-fired capacity proposed for datacentre sites in the United States, the UK and mainland Europe. That is almost four times Great Britain's total peak electricity demand. About 90 per cent of the planned on-site plants are in the United States, where they would emit roughly 345 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent a year if built. Developers cite delays connecting to national grids. UK datacentre projects made more than 100 inquiries to connect to gas in 2024-25, and the UK chancellor has backed temporary on-site fossil generation in the sector's covenant. The research expects datacentre electricity demand to reach 2,354 terawatt-hours by 2035, about 7 per cent of global demand.
- **Why it matters:** The grid queue is now the binding constraint on the buildout, and the answer being financed is generation behind the meter. For AUNZ operators the consequence is ordering: the question for a developer is no longer the power purchase agreement but whether the site connects to the grid at all. Emissions land on the host jurisdiction's account rather than the operator's, which is the same gap the NSW upper-house inquiry identified in planning law, where projects are assessed one at a time while the cumulative load arrives at one substation.
- **Source:** [The Guardian — AI datacentres plan on-site power stations on huge scale despite climate risks, research reveals](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/11/ai-datacentres-on-site-gas-fired-power-stations)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Corroborated
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (independent research house reported by a masthead, with the analyst quoted on the record; the 181GW is proposed capacity, not built capacity, and the emissions figure is an estimate)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-11

### 2. Qantas cut 850 corporate jobs and handed the work to Accenture, citing AI

- **Summary:** **Qantas** will cut 850 corporate roles over the coming years and outsource finance, procurement and marketing work to Accenture, mostly in India. The first changes are due by mid-2027. Chief executive Vanessa Hudson told staff the airline runs on more than 1,000 legacy applications and that the deal would let it accelerate its use of AI. The Australian Services Union said it had been told months earlier there was no plan to offshore 1,000 jobs. Qantas posted a $1.29 billion statutory profit last year and its shares rose more than 6 per cent on the news. The cuts follow 2,500 at NAB and 3,500 at ANZ in the past year.
- **Why it matters:** This is the shape Australian AI-driven offshoring is taking: the stated cause is legacy systems, the stated benefit is AI, and the mechanism is an outsourcing contract with a global integrator. The big four bank chiefs face two days of parliamentary questions on the same pattern from Thursday, and the committee chair has raised directors' insurance as the question boards have not priced. For AUNZ employers, the union's account is the exposure: what Qantas told its workforce will be the evidence base at any future inquiry.

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. Google put its strongest model on general release at the cheaper model's price

- **Summary:** **Google** made Gemini 4 Carbon available to developers, enterprises and consumers on 10 November. It describes the model as its strongest for cybersecurity capability and long-horizon agentic work. Google says it ranks first on CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity's WANDR and Zapier's AutomationBench, at less than half the cost per task of the next best external model. It costs $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens until 30 November, then $4 and $20. The company also previewed /swarm in Antigravity, which splits one prompt across a tracked team of agents with adversarial verifiers. Two weeks earlier the model went to selected cyber defenders under the Fairwind program.
- **Why it matters:** A frontier model at a mid-tier price resets the cost assumptions behind every AUNZ build decision made this quarter. The benchmark scores are the vendor's, run on suites its partners publish, so treat the ranking as a claim and the price as the fact. The /swarm preview is the more consequential release: it is a mainstream product built around a team of agents rather than one, which is precisely the class of system the government's proposed incident-reporting duty was written for.
- **Source:** [Google — Gemini 4 Carbon is now available](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-carbon-now-available/)
