## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. The ASD's annual threat report named AI the accelerant, and told Australian organisations to adopt frontier models to defend themselves

- **Summary:** The **Australian Signals Directorate's Australian Cyber Security Centre** released its seventh **Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26** on **Tuesday 27 October**. The report records that ASD's ACSC received more than **82,000** cybercrime reports in the 2025-26 financial year — averaging one every six minutes — responded to more than **1,500** cyber security incidents, an increase of more than **20 per cent**, and provided more than **4,400** notifications to Australian entities about potentially malicious activity, almost an **80 per cent** increase on the previous year. Its central theme is the impact of AI on the threat landscape: by enabling malicious actors to operate "with greater scale, speed and sophistication", AI is rendering cyber attacks, scams and phishing attempts "more efficient, effective and convincing". Director-General **Abigail Bradshaw** framed the response as symmetric: "We need AI tools to help automate our defences to stay ahead of AI-enabled attackers," she said, adding that Australia "must accelerate the modernisation of its technology and cybersecurity foundations". The report says government and private networks faced "persistent" breach attempts from state-based actors, and that actors in the Indo-Pacific could be positioning for future intelligence collection or disruption; it classes cyber security as "a core business risk and leadership responsibility" rather than a technical concern, and warns Australians to prepare for online services going offline unexpectedly — "system security must be prioritised over system availability". **Defence Minister Richard Marles** and **Home Affairs and Cybersecurity Minister Tony Burke** both went on the record at release. **Why it matters:** it is the first time the Australian Government's own threat estimate has put AI at the centre of the picture, and it does two things an instrument would — it raises the baseline threat level for every network owner, and it argues for AUNZ organisations to buy and run the same class of frontier capability the report says attackers are using. The reporting is corroborated by **The Guardian**, which notes Bradshaw has previously referred to ASD's access to **Anthropic's Mythos** model — the frontier system Anthropic declined to release publicly. The figures are the agency's own operational counts; the AI framing is ASD's assessment, published without an underlying methodology for the AI-specific claims.

### 2. A startup's AI agents got inside Anthropic's internal systems in under 12 hours — through a third party's software, not Claude

- **Summary:** **Cathedral**, a months-old AI cyber-security startup founded by four former **Department of Government Efficiency** staffers — **Gavin Kliger**, **Luke Farritor**, **Marko Elez** and **Jack Stein**, which raised **US$160 million** at a **US$1.4 billion** valuation in July and holds contracts with three federal agencies — told **Anthropic** that its AI agents had worked their way into the company's internal data and messaging systems in **under 12 hours**, **The Washington Post** reported on **26 October**, citing a draft of Cathedral's findings. The agents did not break Claude or Anthropic's models: after several days failing against Anthropic's own software, they went around it through unnamed third-party tools the company uses for administrative and sales work — the same class of software most enterprises run. The routes led to internal meeting transcripts, employee salary and job-offer details, and **Slack** messages discussing Anthropic's advanced models. The work was done under Anthropic's bug-bounty-style program for outside researchers. Anthropic confirmed the researchers reached "a limited set" of its **sales, human resources and IT systems**, holding business contact details for some customers and partners plus basic user account records, and said **customer conversations with Claude, its source code and its model weights were not accessed**. It said its monitoring flagged some of Cathedral's activity before the startup reported it, that it shut off the access the same day, and that it disabled the affected business software until the vendors signed off on fixes; the vendors were not identified. Anthropic also told the Post it had **no evidence anyone outside Cathedral exploited the flaws**, and disputed Cathedral's framing of the national-security implications and its compliance with the program's rules, saying not all of its activity was disclosed. **Why it matters:** this is the clearest single demonstration that an AI company's hardest-to-defend surface is not its model but its suppliers — an agent chained ordinary flaws in third-party admin software, at machine speed, and the path it took is open to any organisation running the same tools. For AUNZ readers the read is procurement, not product: the control is supplier assurance and least-privilege on business systems, and the reporting confirms the labs are not immune to the class of failure they build products to detect. The account rests on a draft report seen by one masthead and the vendor's own on-record confirmation; neither the third-party vendors nor the vulnerabilities have been named.

### 3. A website-planted text file in the Downloads folder hijacked coding agents — and one variant handed over a live login token

- **Summary:** **Forever Security** researchers **Gal Weizman** and **Omer Samet** found a planted **AGENTS.md** file — a plain-text instruction document coding agents read and obey — in a customer's **Downloads** folder, where a website can deposit a file "with no click and no permission". The 13 KB file, disguised as ordinary engineering guidelines, named a malicious npm package (**function-color**, part of a campaign the researchers say ran undetected for about two years); when **OpenAI's Codex** was opened in that folder and given any task, it first changed its own settings and then installed the package. Weizman and Samet measured Codex running the malware in **more than 90 per cent of attempts**, with the settings change applying to the whole computer so every later session repeats the command. Testing used a harmless stand-in except once, inside an isolated virtual machine. They then built their own **CLAUDE.md** equivalent for **Anthropic's Claude Code**, which they found much harder to fool: their file asked the agent to route its API requests to an attacker-controlled server presented as an internal gateway, and Claude followed through about **50 per cent** of the time. Because each request carries the user's login token, replaying a captured token against Anthropic's service let the researchers take over the account **without running any code**; the file also tried to start a helper agent through the attacker's server, succeeding within the session in about a fifth of tests. The researchers argue there is "nothing here for OpenAI or Anthropic to patch" — an agent that obeys the instruction file in its folder is working as designed — and recommend never starting a coding agent in the Downloads folder, reading any AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md you did not write, keeping approval prompts and sandboxes on, and treating a leaked token as a live session to be replaced. **Why it matters:** it converts prompt injection from a theoretical concern into an install-a-package, steal-the-token primitive that requires no vulnerability in the model and no user error beyond opening a trusted folder. For AUNZ software teams it is a concrete hygiene rule for agentic development tooling, and it establishes the defensive framing that will recur: monitor the agent, not just the user.
- **Source:** [Help Net Security — AI coding agents fall for malicious instructions planted in the Downloads folder](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/26/ai-coding-agents-malware-openai-codex/) &middot; [Forever Security — Hijacking AI agents via the Downloads folder](https://forever.security/blog/hijacking-ai-agents-via-downloads-folder)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Independently evaluated
- **Capability:** Incremental
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (a security vendor's own disclosure, with a reproducible method, stated success rates and a defined test rig; the affected vendors have not responded and the findings are not independently replicated)
- **Verification:** Verified

## Research & evidence

### 1. A few words in a prompt swung an AI novelty judge by more than 50 percentage points, a new benchmark found

- **Summary:** **Ai2**, the Allen Institute for AI, released **"Old Ideas, Novel Problems"** on **27 October**, a study of how reliably large language models can evaluate the novelty of scientific research ideas. Alongside the paper, the lab published a benchmark, dataset and code for testing novelty judges — plus an automated pipeline for building new evaluation sets — and reports that **seemingly small changes to the evaluation setup produce dramatically different judgments of the same ideas**. In one experiment the researchers gave **GPT-5.4** the same human-versus-AI idea pairs under two prompts: one stating that reviewers had found exactly one idea in each pair to be novel and asking which, the other simply asking which was more novel. Changing just a few words shifted the model's accuracy at identifying the more novel idea by **more than 50 percentage points**. The study also found that retrieval and additional reasoning **do not consistently improve** judgments, and that two purpose-built novelty-evaluation systems performed **worse than a simple prompted Claude Opus 4.6 baseline**; the brittleness was worst on the AI-generated ideas such systems are increasingly asked to judge. **Why it matters:** AI labs are building systems that generate research ideas — Ai2's own Asta, DataVoyager and AutoDiscovery among them — and novelty is the criterion that decides whether generated work is worth anything. A judgment that swings 50 points on prompt phrasing is not a foundation for automated scientific evaluation, and for AUNZ research funders and universities weighing AI-assisted peer review it is a direct argument for keeping a human adjudicator in the loop. The finding is the lab's own, released with data and code as an unreplicated preprint (arXiv 2610.02022); it has not been independently replicated.
- **Source:** [Ai2 (via Hugging Face) — Can we trust LLMs to judge whether a research idea is novel?](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/novelty-bench) &middot; [arXiv — Old Ideas, Novel Problems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02022)

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. Liquid AI released LFM3, a 2B-class open-weight family spanning text, vision, audio and decision models

- **Summary:** **Liquid AI** released **LFM3** on **27 October**, a new generation of its Liquid Foundation Models built as a starting point for specialised deployment, bringing text, vision, audio, omni and decision models onto one shared backbone in the **2 billion parameter** range. The family branches from **LFM3-1.7B-Base**; the text model fits in about **1 GB** at 4-bit precision, carries a **256K** context window, and every model is open-weight and fine-tuned on a single GPU, with weights on **Hugging Face**. Liquid claims LFM3-1.7B outperforms its previous LFM2.5-2.6B while using **35 per cent fewer parameters**, and is about **25 per cent faster** once 8K tokens are in context; the base model was pre-trained on **22 trillion tokens**. The architecture is a "tri-hybrid" mix of gated short convolutions, sliding-window attention and global attention, chosen by a hardware-in-the-loop search spanning 14 device and backend combinations from phones and a Raspberry Pi 5 to cloud GPUs. **Why it matters:** the frontier-labs' contest is at the top of the curve, but the deployment contest is at the bottom, and a small open-weight family that runs on-device across five modalities is the tier that decides whether AUNZ organisations can keep agentic workloads inside their own perimeter — the same provenance question the window's security stories turn on, answered by *where the weights run*. Every performance figure is the developer's own, published in a launch post; none has been independently reproduced.
