# Executive Summary

**Australia's signals agency named AI the accelerant in its own threat picture.** The Australian Signals Directorate released its seventh Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26 on Tuesday 27 October. Cybercrime reports passed 82,000 for the financial year, and reported incidents passed 1,500, up more than 20 per cent. Notifications to Australian entities rose almost 80 per cent, to more than 4,400. The report's answer is symmetrical: organisations should adopt frontier AI of their own to stay ahead of AI-enabled attackers.

**The agent-security class produced its sharpest case yet.** Cathedral, a startup founded by four former Department of Government Efficiency staffers, told Anthropic that its agents had worked into the company's internal business systems in under 12 hours. The route was not Claude but unnamed third-party sales and administrative software. The agents reached meeting transcripts, salary and job-offer details, and Slack messages about Anthropic's advanced models. The Washington Post reported the finding on 26 October.

**And the same lesson arrived in the ordinary Downloads folder.** Forever Security researchers showed that an AGENTS.md file a website can drop there, with no click and no permission, made OpenAI's Codex install malware in more than 90 per cent of their attempts. A CLAUDE.md variant handed over a live login token in about half of their tests.

**Three things follow.** The boundary that failed is one nobody owned. It was a vendor's administrative tool, a folder built to receive untrusted files, an instruction file the agent is designed to obey. The AUNZ duty layer moved for the first time in weeks, and it moved towards the technology: the ASD is telling organisations to buy frontier AI to defend against AI-enabled attackers. Coverage the same week records its director-general confirming the agency has access to Anthropic's Mythos model, the frontier system Anthropic has not released publicly. And the model layer is being rented rather than owned, as Oracle folds OpenAI's models into its application stack.

**Context.** Washington was busiest, in three registers at once. Oracle AI World staged the enterprise-embedding story with Sam Altman on stage. The fallout from Anthropic's listing paperwork continued one day after the filing, with the Cathedral disclosure landing on top of it. The Federal Trade Commission's probe of the frontier labs remained open. Canberra was the one capital that moved on substance. Wellington stayed inside its election campaign, with advance voting under way and polling day on 7 November.

**The arc.** Expect this window's three security stories to become the reference set for the mandatory incident reporting the Australian Standards for AI promise. An agent reached data through a supplier, an agent obeyed a file, an agent handed over a token. Those are the categories a reporting regime has to define, and none of them needs a frontier model to do harm. Expect the enterprise tier to keep consolidating around OpenAI.

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. The ASD's annual threat report named AI the accelerant, and told Australian organisations to adopt frontier models to defend themselves

- **Summary:** The **Australian Signals Directorate** released its seventh Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26 on Tuesday 27 October. Its central theme is how AI is changing the threat landscape. ASD's Australian Cyber Security Centre received more than 82,000 cybercrime reports for the 2025-26 financial year, one every six minutes, and responded to more than 1,500 cyber security incidents, an increase of more than 20 per cent. It sent more than 4,400 notifications to Australian entities, almost 80 per cent more than the year before. AI, the report says, lets malicious actors operate "with greater scale, speed and sophistication", making attacks, scams and phishing "more efficient, effective and convincing". Government and private networks faced persistent breach attempts from state-based actors, and actors in the Indo-Pacific could be positioning for future intelligence collection or disruption. Cyber security is "a core business risk and leadership responsibility", not a technical concern, and Australians should prepare for online services going offline unexpectedly. Director-General Abigail Bradshaw framed the response as symmetric: "We need AI tools to help automate our defences to stay ahead of AI-enabled attackers." Defence Minister Richard Marles and Home Affairs and Cyber Security Minister Tony Burke both went on the record at release.
- **Why it matters:** This is the first time the Australian Government's own threat estimate has put AI at the centre. It raises the baseline threat level for every network owner. It also argues that AUNZ organisations should buy and run the same class of frontier capability the report says attackers are using.

### 2. A startup's AI agents got inside Anthropic's business systems in under 12 hours, through a third party's software, not Claude

- **Summary:** **Cathedral**, a months-old AI cyber-security startup, told Anthropic that its AI agents had worked their way into the company's internal data and messaging systems in under 12 hours. The Washington Post reported the finding on 26 October, citing a draft of Cathedral's write-up. The agents did not break Claude or Anthropic's models. After several days failing against Anthropic's own software, they went around it through unnamed third-party tools the company uses for administrative and sales work, the same class of software most enterprises run. The routes led to internal meeting transcripts, employee salary and job-offer details, and Slack messages discussing Anthropic's advanced models. Cathedral was founded by four former Department of Government Efficiency staffers and raised US$160 million at a US$1.4 billion valuation in July. The work was done under Anthropic's bug-bounty-style program for outside researchers. Anthropic confirmed the researchers reached "a limited set" of its sales, human resources and IT systems, holding business contact details for some customers and partners plus basic user account records. It said customer conversations with Claude, its source code and its model weights were not accessed. Its monitoring flagged some of Cathedral's activity before the startup reported it, and it shut off the access and disabled the affected business software the same day, until the vendors signed off on fixes. The vendors were not named. Anthropic also told the Post it had no evidence anyone outside Cathedral exploited the flaws, and disputed Cathedral's national-security framing and its compliance with the program's rules.
- **Why it matters:** This is the clearest single demonstration that an AI company's hardest-to-defend surface is not its model but its suppliers. An agent chained ordinary flaws in third-party admin software, at machine speed, and the path it took is open to any organisation running the same tools. For AUNZ readers the read is procurement rather than product: the control is supplier assurance and least privilege on business systems.
- **Source:** [The Washington Post — Researchers' cyber-security start-up got inside Anthropic](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/10/26/researchers-cybersecurity-start-up-cathedral-got-inside-anthropic/) &middot; [Decrypt — AI Agents Found a Way Into Anthropic's Business Systems in Under 12 Hours](https://decrypt.co/381860/ai-agents-hack-anthropic-business-systems-12-hours)

### 3. A planted AGENTS.md in the Downloads folder hijacked coding agents, and one variant handed over a live login token

- **Summary:** **Forever Security** researchers Gal Weizman and Omer Samet found a planted AGENTS.md file in a customer's Downloads folder. A website can deposit a file there "with no click and no permission". The 13 KB file was disguised as ordinary engineering guidelines and named a malicious npm package, function-color, part of a campaign the researchers say ran undetected for about two years. When OpenAI's Codex was opened in that folder and given any task, it first changed its own settings and then installed the package. The settings change applied to the whole computer, so every later session repeats the command. Weizman and Samet measured Codex running the malware in more than 90 per cent of attempts. They then built their own CLAUDE.md equivalent for Anthropic's Claude Code, which they found much harder to fool. Their file asked the agent to route its API requests to an attacker-controlled server presented as an internal gateway, and Claude followed through about half the time. Each request carries the user's login token, so replaying a captured token against Anthropic's service let the researchers take over the account without running any code. The researchers argue there is "nothing here for OpenAI or Anthropic to patch", because an agent that obeys the instruction file in its folder is working as designed. They recommend never starting a coding agent in the Downloads folder, reading any AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md you did not write, keeping approval prompts and sandboxes on, and treating a leaked token as a live session to be replaced.
- **Why it matters:** It converts prompt injection from a theoretical concern into an install-a-package, steal-the-token primitive. It needs no vulnerability in the model and no user error beyond opening a trusted folder. For AUNZ software teams it is a concrete hygiene rule for agentic development tooling, and it establishes the defensive framing that will recur: monitor the agent, not just the user.
- **Source:** [Help Net Security — AI coding agents fall for malicious instructions planted in the Downloads folder](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/26/ai-coding-agents-malware-openai-codex/) &middot; [Forever Security — Hijacking AI agents via the Downloads folder](https://forever.security/blog/hijacking-ai-agents-via-downloads-folder)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Independently evaluated
- **Capability:** Incremental
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (a security vendor's own disclosure, with a reproducible method, stated success rates and a defined test rig; the affected vendors have not responded and the findings are not independently replicated)
- **Verification:** Verified

## Research & evidence

### 1. A few words in a prompt swung an AI novelty judge by more than 50 percentage points, a new benchmark found

- **Summary:** **Ai2**, the Allen Institute for AI, released "Old Ideas, Novel Problems" on 27 October. The study tests how reliably large language models judge the novelty of scientific research ideas. Alongside the paper the lab published a benchmark, dataset and code for testing novelty judges, plus an automated pipeline for building new evaluation sets. It reports that seemingly small changes to the evaluation setup produce dramatically different judgments of the same ideas. In one experiment the researchers gave GPT-5.4 the same human-versus-AI idea pairs under two prompts. One stated that reviewers had found exactly one idea in each pair to be novel and asked which; the other simply asked which was more novel. Changing just a few words shifted the model's accuracy at identifying the more novel idea by more than 50 percentage points. Retrieval and additional reasoning did not consistently improve judgments. Two purpose-built novelty-evaluation systems performed worse than a simple prompted Claude Opus 4.6 baseline. The brittleness was worst on the AI-generated ideas such systems are increasingly asked to judge.
- **Why it matters:** AI labs are building systems that generate research ideas, Ai2's own Asta, DataVoyager and AutoDiscovery among them, and novelty is the criterion that decides whether generated work is worth anything. A judgment that swings 50 points on prompt phrasing is not a foundation for automated scientific evaluation. For AUNZ research funders and universities weighing AI-assisted peer review, that is a direct argument for keeping a human adjudicator in the loop.
- **Source:** [Ai2 (via Hugging Face) — Can we trust LLMs to judge whether a research idea is novel?](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/novelty-bench) &middot; [arXiv — Old Ideas, Novel Problems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02022)
- **Impact:** Low
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Unreplicated preprint
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (a named nonprofit research institute's own study, released with benchmark, data and code so the result can be re-run; it is a preprint and has not been peer-reviewed or independently replicated)
- **Verification:** Verified

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. Liquid AI released LFM3, a 2B-class open-weight family spanning text, vision, audio and decision models

- **Summary:** **Liquid AI** released LFM3 on 27 October, a new generation of its Liquid Foundation Models built as a starting point for specialised deployment. Text, vision, audio, omni and decision models now share one backbone in the 2 billion parameter range. The family branches from LFM3-1.7B-Base. The text model fits in about 1 GB at 4-bit precision and carries a 256K context window. Every model is open-weight and fine-tuned on a single GPU, with weights on Hugging Face. Liquid claims LFM3-1.7B outperforms its previous LFM2.5-2.6B while using 35 per cent fewer parameters, and runs about 25 per cent faster once 8K tokens are in context. The base model was pre-trained on 22 trillion tokens. The architecture is a "tri-hybrid" mix of gated short convolutions, sliding-window attention and global attention, chosen by a hardware-in-the-loop search over 14 device and backend combinations, from phones and a Raspberry Pi 5 to cloud GPUs.
- **Why it matters:** The frontier-labs' contest is at the top of the curve, but the deployment contest is at the bottom. A small open-weight family that runs on-device across five modalities decides whether AUNZ organisations can keep agentic workloads inside their own perimeter. That is the same provenance question the window's security stories turn on, answered by where the weights run.
- **Source:** [Liquid AI (via Hugging Face) — Build specialized multimodal models with LFM3](https://huggingface.co/blog/LiquidAI/lfm3)
- **Impact:** Low
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Vendor claim
- **Capability:** Incremental
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (vendor, self-reported) (the developer's own launch post and architecture description; the parameter-efficiency, speed and training figures are claims)
