## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Hundreds of the AI hacking tool behind recent intrusions were left open to anyone

- **Summary:** **Censys** found more than 800 deployments of Artex reachable from the internet without a password. Artex is an open-source penetration-testing tool built from AI agents, released in July. Microsoft says it carries an attack from reconnaissance to data exfiltration with little human input. CrowdStrike tied one Artex instance to the September attacks on South Korean financial firms. Microsoft tied the tool to October's ConnectWise breach. Censys researcher Silas Cutler said many instances belong to authorised testers. But the software does not need to be internet-facing, and an exposed instance also exposes the implants and attack data it holds. Artex has since gone closed-source, though copies of its code still circulate.
- **Why it matters:** The tool that made agentic attacks cheap has become a surface in its own right. That is the control lesson for AUNZ defenders: an agent framework is a privileged workload, not a developer utility. Inventory it, authenticate it, and keep it off the public internet. The governance lesson is quieter. Nobody regulates a penetration-testing agent, and nobody had to, because the exposure came from operators rather than the vendor.
- **Source:** [Censys — Artex: agentic asbestos](https://censys.com/blog/artex-agentic-asbestos/) &middot; [TechCrunch — Hundreds of Artex AI hacking tools found publicly exposed to the web](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/hundreds-of-artex-ai-hacking-tools-found-publicly-exposed-to-the-web/)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (a scanning specialist's own measurement, reported by TechCrunch, and consistent with Microsoft's and CrowdStrike's published attributions)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-06

### 2. OpenAI hired the researchers who broke into it, and gave them its cyber models

- **Summary:** **OpenAI** said on 6 November that it had formed a partnership with Hacktron. The two will choose high-priority areas to test together, focused on how OpenAI's systems interact with third-party services. Hacktron's access to frontier models expands through Daybreak Red. OpenAI said it met the firm through its bug bounty program. Hacktron is a small team that used a rival lab's model to reach ChatGPT and Codex accounts through a single sign-on flaw on OpenAI's community forum. That work was reported in September.
- **Why it matters:** This is the disclosure layer doing its job one step late. An outsider found the exposure first, and the vendor's answer was to buy the capability. The scope is the part worth copying. The risk sat where the model met a third-party service, not inside the model. Any AUNZ organisation wiring agents into SaaS should test that seam first.
- **Source:** [OpenAI — Partnering with Hacktron to find vulnerabilities in OpenAI's systems](https://openai.com/index/partnering-with-hacktron) &middot; [CBS News — 3 guys hacked OpenAI using a rival Anthropic model](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/openai-hack-anthropic-claude-vulnerabilities/)
- **Impact:** Guarded

- **Obligation ref:** None

## Regulation & obligation

### 1. A NSW inquiry found the approvals system cannot see the harm data centres do together

- **Summary:** **NSW's** upper-house Public Accountability and Works Committee tabled its data centre report on 5 November. It makes 26 recommendations after 143 submissions and five hearings. It found the planning framework assesses each project on its own, while water, power and traffic effects are cumulative. Members voted 6&ndash;1 to remove recommendations for a state and national moratorium. Chair Abigail Boyd, a Greens MLC, called for a moratorium anyway, and said industry and the NSW Government had refused to appear as witnesses. On 6 November Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles said data centres should not be built in the suburbs, as reports said federal Labor was preparing a ban. A Sydney project involving NextDC and OpenAI is awaiting approval.
- **Why it matters:** Siting is becoming the binding constraint on Australian AI capacity, ahead of chips or capital. A pause on new approvals now has majority support in one house of one parliament, and the federal signals point the same way before any bill exists. AUNZ operators should read a planning approval as the scarce asset in the chain, and a community objection as the most likely cause of delay.
- **Source:** [NSW Parliament — Inquiry into data centres: report tabled (media release)](https://files.parliament.nsw.gov.au/fileapi/ParlFiles/GetArtifact?serverRelativeUrl=%2Flcdocs%2Fother%2F24720%2FMedia%20Release%20-%20Report%20Tabled%20Data%20Centres%20-%205%20November%202026.pdf) &middot; [Reuters — Australia data centre inquiry finds inadequate checks, stops short of moratorium](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/australia-data-centre-inquiry-finds-inadequate-checks-stops-short-moratorium-2026-11-05/) &middot; [InnovationAus — Marles rules out new suburban data centres](https://www.innovationaus.com/marles-rules-out-new-suburban-data-centres/)

- **Obligation:** Signalled
- **Obligation ref:** None yet — the 26 recommendations bind nothing; they target the NSW planning framework under the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (the committee's own media release; Reuters reported the report and the vote; Marles' remark is reported behind InnovationAus' paywall and not independently checked here)

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. Enterprise AI spending flattened even as usage kept climbing

- **Summary:** **Ramp** reported that token spend across its corporate card base grew 1.5 per cent across August and September combined. It had grown 42 per cent in July. Usage kept rising over the same period. The top 1 per cent of firms, which were spending close to US$10,000 per employee a month at their summer peak, slipped below it. Median firms spend US$12 to $15 per employee a month. Ramp's lead economist Ara Kharazian said almost all AI spending comes from venture-backed, high-growth companies.
- **Why it matters:** Revenue at the frontier labs has been underwritten by a spend curve that just bent. One payments platform is not the market, and its sample leans towards startups. If the pattern holds, price cuts are already doing work that growth was doing. Anthropic's share sale is valued on the other assumption.
- **Source:** [Ramp — AI Index](https://ramp.com/data/ai-index) &middot; [TechCrunch — Ramp data shows AI spending stalling just as Anthropic nears its IPO](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/ramp-data-shows-ai-spending-stalling-just-as-anthropic-nears-its-ipo/)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Corroborated
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (a single payments platform's transaction data, reported with its own economist; it is not an independent market measure)
- **Verification:** Verified

### 2. An OpenAI survey found Australian students building agents faster than their universities can assess them

- **Summary:** **OpenAI** published on 5 November that UNSW, the University of Sydney, La Trobe and Adelaide University have agreements giving students and staff access to its models. It also released a survey of more than 1,000 Australian university students that it commissioned. Four in five said they use AI several times a week. More than a quarter said they had built an agent or assistant to manage study, work or personal life. Nearly four in five wanted more chances to use advanced AI on real-world projects. The post carries no assessment-integrity data.
- **Why it matters:** Adoption is running ahead of the rules that would make it legible. The fact with consequences is undergraduates building agents, because an agent that acts is harder to attribute than an essay that is written. Read the survey as a vendor's market document. Then plan for the behaviour it describes, because the procurement is already done.
- **Source:** [OpenAI — Australian universities are creating AI-native graduates](https://openai.com/index/australian-universities-ai-native-graduates)
- **Impact:** Low
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. A startup put a text-to-speech model in 32 megabytes and ran it in a browser

- **Summary:** **Underdog** released Woof, a text-to-speech model it measures at 31.7 megabytes. The demo is a browser game in which every voice is generated on the device. The company posted it on 5 November. The claim is size and local execution rather than benchmark position. No independent evaluation of output quality has been published.
- **Why it matters:** The cost of capability is falling at the small end as fast as it is rising at the large one. A speech model that ships inside a web page changes the privacy argument, because audio never leaves the device. It also changes the economics for AUNZ developers who cannot buy GPU capacity. Treat the quality claim as unproven until someone measures it.
- **Source:** [Underdog — Escape (Woof demo)](https://underdog.ai/escape) &middot; [TechCrunch — Underdog shows off a 32MB speech model in new demo](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/underdog-shows-off-a-32mb-speech-model-in-new-demo/)
