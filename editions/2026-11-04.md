## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Britain's cyber agency expects AI-written malware inside an attack on critical infrastructure by 2028

- **Summary:** The **National Cyber Security Centre** published its annual review, State of the Cyber Nation, on 3 November. It assesses that AI-powered malware is "highly likely" to have appeared in a ransomware attack on UK critical national infrastructure by 2028. The report says Russia and China will use AI routinely in cyber operations. It puts the annual cost of malicious cyber activity to UK organisations at £14.7 billion. Richard Horne, the agency's chief executive, said the danger is not that AI invents new ways to attack. It is that weaknesses tolerated for years can now be found and exploited at a scale that was once unimaginable.
- **Why it matters:** The claim is a forecast by a government agency, not an observed event, and should be read that way. The diagnosis is the part that travels. End-of-life systems, default passwords and unpatched services are now discoverable faster than they can be fixed. Australia's own cyber agency made a comparable warning in October. Neither assessment creates a duty, so the decision to spend still sits with each board, which is the gap both agencies are describing.
- **Source:** [NCSC — Cyber chief: UK facing AI-powered attacks on critical infrastructure within two years](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/news/cyber-chief-uk-facing-ai-powered-attacks-on-critical-infrastructure-in-two-years) &middot; [NCSC — State of the Cyber Nation: Annual Review 2026](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/annual-review-2026) &middot; [The Guardian — AI-powered malware 'highly likely' to be used to hack UK infrastructure by 2028](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/03/ai-powered-malware-artificial-intelligence-hacking-critical-uk-infrastructure)

## Regulation & obligation

### 1. WA Police scanned 1.38 million faces in a five-month trial and made 122 arrests

- **Summary:** Western Australia Police ended a trial of overt live facial recognition on 31 October. Cameras on marked vans scanned 1,380,882 faces across 100 deployments at 43 locations, including the Perth royal show. The force reported 122 arrests, contact with 146 registered sex offenders and 10 welfare checks. Nine alerts were wrong, which police put at 0.00065 per cent of scans. Commissioner Col Blanch said each false match led to a conversation and no arrest. Images of people who were not on the alert list were pixelated in real time and not stored, police said.
- **Why it matters:** The force divided errors by scans. Civil Liberties Australia argues the honest divisor is the people stopped, and that the true rate is only known once the 122 arrests are tested in court. That distinction decides whether live facial matching is a targeted tool or a dragnet with a small tail of mistakes. The trial is now the reference case for any AUNZ agency weighing the same deployment, and the arithmetic is the first thing that will be tested.
- **Source:** [The Guardian — Police use facial recognition to scan one million faces in WA trial leading to 122 arrests](https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2026/nov/02/wa-police-facial-recognition-technology-trial)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (figures given by the force at a press conference and reported by Guardian Australia; the civil liberties criticism is quoted, and the conviction rate behind the 122 arrests is not yet known)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-02

### 2. A Crown prosecutor cited a case that does not exist after an AI search

- **Summary:** A senior Crown Prosecution Service prosecutor cited a nonexistent precedent in a High Court case, the Guardian reported on 3 November. Ravinder Dhillon referred to a decision she called R v J [2024] EWCA Crim 3529 while arguing that an 11-year-old boy should be prosecuted for raping his younger sister. The Guardian understands the citation came from an AI response during an internet search. Two High Court judges called her explanation unsatisfactory. The court said the error did not change the outcome of the boy's challenge.
- **Why it matters:** A model inventing a citation is ordinary. What matters is that a state prosecutor used one in a document arguing for a child's prosecution, and that no review caught it before a judge did. The Guardian reports dozens of AI-related errors in court proceedings this year, mostly from people representing themselves. For AUNZ legal teams the control is unglamorous and cheap: check every authority against a primary database, whether or not a model drafted the document.
- **Source:** [The Guardian — 'A serious failure': CPS prosecutor cites imaginary case to high court after AI search](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/03/a-serious-failure-cps-prosecutor-cites-imaginary-case-to-high-court-after-ai-search) &middot; [High Court of England and Wales — judgment in the Administrative Court, 2026 EWHC 2778](https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWHC/Admin/2026/2778.html)

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. A preprint says frontier models can read raw model weights, and a laptop model can too

- **Summary:** Four authors posted a paper on 2 November reporting that language models can interpret and rewrite the weights of a neural network. They call the practice weight-space programming. They report that GPT-6 Astra identified what a small network had been trained to do from its weights alone. They also say it lifted Qwen3.5-0.8B's MATH-500 score from 7.9 to 48.1 per cent by editing 0.016 per cent of its parameters. Simon Willison reproduced part of the result on 3 November, using Qwen 3.8 27B on a laptop.
- **Why it matters:** The critiques are as instructive as the claim. Matthew Honnibal says the paper conflates reading weights with running code to change them, and Sean Goedecke argues the worked example repeats the steps a human researcher would take. The question worth watching is the narrower one: can a model infer behaviour from numbers, with no tools? If that holds, capability becomes something an auditor can read out of a file, and the same is true for anyone publishing open weights.
- **Source:** [Rikiya Takehi et al. — Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: LLMs can natively read & write neural network weights](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/) &middot; [Simon Willison — Qwen 3.8 27B on my laptop can interpret model weights, too](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/3/qwen-can-interpret-model-weights/)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Unreplicated preprint
- **Capability:** Material
- **Reliability:** Tier 3/4 — Specialist (an authors' preprint with no peer review; one partial independent reproduction and two published methodological critiques, from Honnibal and Goedecke)
- **Verification:** Verified

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. Drax signed a £20 billion agreement to put a gigawatt datacentre on its power station estate

- **Summary:** **Drax** said on 3 November that it had signed an agreement with Brookfield and Radiant, Brookfield's AI infrastructure platform, to develop a datacentre on the Drax Power Station estate in North Yorkshire. The project targets more than 1 gigawatt and could attract more than £20 billion of investment. If built in full, Drax says it would be one of the largest datacentres in the United Kingdom. Drax will provide land and access to existing grid connections. Its shares rose almost 9 per cent, to a market value near £3 billion.
- **Why it matters:** The site is Britain's largest single emitter of carbon dioxide, and its subsidy halves from 2027, so the plant needs a second income. A datacentre turns land and a grid connection into revenue without joining a connection queue. The announcement carried no financial detail and no final investment decision, which is the gap between a share price move and a project. The AUNZ parallel is direct, because Australian data centre rules are being written around this same trade of power access for local benefit.
- **Source:** [Drax — Powering AI and data centres](https://www.drax.com/what-we-do/ai-data-centres/) &middot; [Yahoo Finance / PA — Drax partners with Brookfield on £20 billion AI data centre project](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/drax-partners-brookfield-20-billion-073600467.html) &middot; [The Guardian — UK's most polluting power plant could power country's largest AI datacentre](https://www.theguardian.com/business/2026/nov/03/uk-drax-power-station-ai-datacentre)

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. Google told US schools that blocking its AI also turns off its Search

- **Summary:** **Google** announced new classroom AI features and new administrator controls on 3 November. Access to Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search will be off for K-12 student accounts until a school administrator turns each one on. A school that withholds consent for the AI products cannot keep Search. Chris Phillips, Google's general manager for education, told the New York Times that search and AI are deeply integrated. New York's mayor, Zohran Mamdani, said Google was using monopoly power to force AI into public schools.
- **Why it matters:** A product decision has become a competition question. If the most widely used search product cannot be separated from its AI layer, a school's refusal is not a real choice and consent becomes nominal. The same bundling arrives in AUNZ procurement, where devices and accounts are bought as one package. Expect the argument to move from product design to whether tying search to AI is an abuse of market power.
- **Source:** [Google — Designing responsible AI for learning](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/education/ai-learning-era-updates-2026/) &middot; [CBS News — Google to end search access for schools that block its AI tools](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/google-search-access-schools-ai-tools/)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (Google's own announcement of the control change; the description of it as coercive is critics' opinion, and the New York Times interview is quoted by other outlets rather than read here)
