## Regulation & obligation

### 1. Australia's AI standards consultation drew 1,533 submissions, mostly from individuals

- **Summary:** The **Office of AI** published its "What we heard" report on 13 November, closing the public consultation on the national AI standards. The consultation, Getting it right: Building AI infrastructure that works for Australia, received 1,533 submissions. Individuals lodged 73 per cent, business and industry 12 per cent, organisations 8 per cent and AI and technology representatives 6 per cent. Of those giving a location, 225 came from New South Wales, 194 from Victoria and 120 from Queensland, and about 2 per cent were international. Energy, water and local impacts were the most significant issues raised about large-scale infrastructure.
- **Why it matters:** The composition is the finding. A standards process drawing three quarters of its responses from the public, rather than from the industry it will regulate, constrains how far the requirements can be shaped around operator cost. The report also records where consensus failed: copyright and training data remain the most divergent subjects. Both sit inside a framework that the Office of AI says will carry mandatory requirements for large data centres on energy and water. For AUNZ operators this is the evidence base the draft standards will cite, and copyright is the question still open.
- **Source:** [PM&C — AI standards shaped by Australians](https://www.pmc.gov.au/news/ai-standards-shaped-australians) &middot; [PM&C — Public consultation insights](https://www.pmc.gov.au/domestic-policy/office-ai/getting-it-right-building-ai-infrastructure-works-australia/public-consultation-insights)

## Research & evidence

### 1. A new benchmark shows models can spot a user's real problem and still answer the wrong one

- **Summary:** **Ai2** released XYBench on 13 November, built with researchers at Carnegie Mellon, NVIDIA and Johns Hopkins. The benchmark holds 8,115 questions framed around the XY problem, where a user asks about an approach that will not achieve their goal. All five models tested were more likely to answer the stated request than the underlying goal. On Stack Overflow questions, solutions to the real goal appeared in 59 to 71 per cent of responses, against 75 to 86 per cent for the stated request. Only 35 to 41 per cent of answers focused on the real goal. The paper won the Best Resource Paper award at EMNLP 2026.
- **Why it matters:** The gap between choosing and writing is the useful result. Shown two candidate answers, the same models picked the goal-focused one in 71 to 84 per cent of cases, then produced that kind of answer far less often. That points at training and prompting rather than at knowledge, and the dataset is published for checking. For anyone putting an assistant in front of users, it says the ordinary failure is compliance with the question asked.
- **Source:** [Hugging Face — XYBench: Helping with the problem behind the question](https://huggingface.co/blog/allenai/xybench)
- **Impact:** Low
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (the laboratory's own benchmark release, with paper, data and code published and the automated judge validated against two human reviewers; the model comparisons are the authors' measurements)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-13

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Australia's cyber agency told organisations not to rely on prompts to contain an AI agent

- **Summary:** The **Australian Signals Directorate** published Securing AI testing environments on 12 November. The guidance covers testing of AI models and agents used in cyber security work, and sets out technical controls, monitoring and governance for containment. It states that prompts and model-level safeguards are not sufficient on their own. The agency cites recent disclosures in which agents reached systems beyond their intended test scope when containment failed. The publication sits alongside its earlier guidance on adopting agentic AI and on defending against AI-enabled attacks.
- **Why it matters:** This is the first AUNZ government publication written for the failure class the industry spent the year documenting. It is guidance, not an instrument, so it creates no duty and no penalty. Its weight comes from procurement and assurance: an Australian agency or bank assessing an agent platform now has a domestic reference saying model-level guardrails are not a control. Human oversight is the mechanism named, and that is the harder thing to evidence.
- **Source:** [ASD — Secure AI testing to reduce unauthorised access](https://www.cyber.gov.au/news/secure-ai-testing-to-reduce-unauthorised-access) &middot; [ASD — Securing AI testing environments](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/securing-ai-testing-environments)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (the agency's own publication and the news item announcing it)
- **Verification:** Verified

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. Microsoft's 365 failure took Copilot and two AI vendors' services down with it

- **Summary:** A **Microsoft 365** outage began at 17:54 GMT on 12 November and ran for more than 22 hours. Teams, Outlook and Copilot were unreachable in at least 70 countries. Microsoft said the fault came from an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade, and that it was "not related to cyber or AI/agents". A spokesperson apologised and promised a full post-incident report. Anthropic said Claude was recovering from errors caused by the outage, and OpenAI reported problems with its Microsoft connectors. New Zealand's immigration service paused visa processing, and COP31 delegates in Antalya lost access to negotiating documents.
- **Why it matters:** The assistant layer inherits the availability of the mail layer. Both leading AI vendors run connectors into Microsoft 365, so one network change upstream took Claude and ChatGPT down alongside the products they integrate with. That dependency is rarely visible in a service-level agreement, and it is the argument for treating identity and mail providers as part of the AI supply chain. Microsoft's insistence that agents were not involved also shows the first question now asked of any outage.
- **Source:** [BBC News — Microsoft says it is 'deeply sorry' for global outage](https://www.bbc.com/news/live/cq5y10ge3dp3t) &middot; [The Guardian — Microsoft outage shakes up companies, schools and local governments](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/13/microsoft-outage-shakes-up-companies-local-governments)

### 2. Eight chip and platform companies took a seat on the open inference engine's roadmap

- **Summary:** **LMSYS Org** launched the SGLang Consortium on 12 November, at the first SGLang Summit in San Francisco. The founding members are NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Google, Meta, Cloudflare, Modal and RadixArk. Each will name a technical representative and attend two roadmap meetings a year, with working groups between them. SGLang's maintainers keep control of technical decisions and code acceptance. The consortium cites Deloitte in saying inference will account for about two thirds of AI compute in 2026, against about half in 2025.
- **Why it matters:** The engine that decides inference cost now has its hardware vendors in the room. That is a governance change before it is a technical one. An open-source project competing with the hyperscalers' own serving stacks has given those vendors standing input, while keeping the maintainers' veto over code. For AUNZ buyers, the roadmap is where support for a given accelerator lands, so the membership list is a signal about which chips will be cheap to serve on.
- **Source:** [LMSYS Org — Announcing the SGLang Consortium](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-11-12-sglang-consortium/)

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. EU leaders made AI a summit subject for the first time and agreed no new measures

- **Summary:** The **European Council** met informally in Dublin on 13 November for its first strategic discussion on artificial intelligence. Arthur Mensch, chief executive of Mistral AI, briefed leaders on competitiveness and AI sovereignty. Geoffrey Hinton briefed them on fundamental rights, children, democratic integrity, labour markets and cybersecurity. President António Costa said catching up with global peers would require significant additional funding, and pointed to the next long-term budget and the European Competitiveness Fund. He named the AI Act as the bloc's existing instrument, and said leaders would return to AI and jobs at a 2027 social summit. No conclusions were adopted.
- **Why it matters:** An informal meeting produces no instrument, so the value lies in what it commits the bloc to fund. The framing has moved from regulating AI to financing it, with market concentration and cybersecurity now on the same agenda as rights. For AUNZ readers, the relevant claim is standards: Costa's assertion that the EU can shape global rules is the basis on which the AI Act travels through procurement and partner agreements.
- **Source:** [European Council — Informal meeting of heads of state or government, 13 November 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/meetings/european-council/2026/11/13/)
