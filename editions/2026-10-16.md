# Executive Summary

**Top stories.** The window's most consequential story was the state turning on the labs, in two jurisdictions at once. On **Wednesday 14 October** the **US Federal Trade Commission** served **civil investigative demands** on **OpenAI** and **Anthropic** — reported by *Axios* on **15 October**, each document running past **50 pages**, seeking material on security incidents and safeguarding practices, on marketing claims and privacy policies, with the agency signalling it will compel executives to testify; the same day, **eight state attorneys-general** — Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia — filed **separate consumer-protection suits** against **OpenAI** alleging it marketed ChatGPT as safe for under-13s while the product harmed minors. Two things follow. First, **the enforcement layer arrived from Washington and the state capitals, not from a new AI statute**: the FTC's demands are the general consumer-protection power applied to AI marketing, and the eight state suits run in state courts under state law — a route the attorneys-general chose explicitly rather than wait for federal rules. Second, **the money question turned from capability to cash**: Anthropic's revenue is now large enough that a single quarter's disclosure moved the AI trade, the KOSPI's circuit breaker was triggered by doubts about the financing of the buildout rather than by any fall in demand, and the labs answered by shipping — Meta's open weights, Nvidia's compute donations, Mistral's small forecasting models — rather than by publishing frontier research.

### 1. The FTC served civil investigative demands on OpenAI and Anthropic, and eight states sued OpenAI
- **Summary:** The **US Federal Trade Commission** sent **civil investigative demands** to **OpenAI** and **Anthropic** on **Wednesday 14 October**, *Axios* reported on **15 October** — documents running **more than 50 pages each** that seek material on **security incidents** and safeguarding practices, plus **marketing materials** and **privacy policies** — and is preparing to compel senior executives from both companies to **testify**. A civil investigative demand is the agency's evidence-gathering instrument ahead of possible litigation, and the FTC's authority here is its general power over **unfair or deceptive** conduct affecting consumers, not any AI-specific statute; *Axios* reported that demands would go to **third-party AI organisations** at a later date. The same day, **eight state attorneys-general** — **Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia** — filed **separate lawsuits** against **OpenAI** in their own state courts, alleging it marketed ChatGPT as safe for children under 13 when it "is absolutely not appropriate for kids of that age", and pointing to the platform's **rogue AI agents** and to cases in which the chatbot allegedly **coached users towards self-harm**. The states seek **disgorgement of profits** earned while minors were harmed, orders to stop the safety claims they call deceptive, product changes, civil penalties and, in some complaints, damages; Montana adds claims under its consumer privacy law over the absence of parental consent for under-13 data. **OpenAI** said it has "industry-leading age-specific protections" and will "engage constructively with the FTC". **Why it matters:** this is the first window in which **general consumer-protection law, rather than an AI instrument**, was pointed squarely at the frontier labs on both the federal and state tracks — and the FTC's stated theory of liability is narrow and testable: that the harm is in what the companies **said about their products**, not in a novel regulatory category. Read the state suits as **commenced but contested**: they are filed complaints stating allegations, OpenAI has not yet answered them, and federal pre-emption and speech defences will be litigated. ## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Two critical flaws in LiteLLM, the LLM gateway enterprises sit behind, were disclosed and patched
- **Summary:** **Check Point Research** disclosed two vulnerabilities in **LiteLLM**, the open-source gateway that organisations place between their applications and AI providers including **OpenAI**, **Anthropic** and **Google** — a component with roughly **97 million downloads a month** and around **59,000 GitHub stars**, and one that is frequently embedded inside other platforms rather than deployed standalone. **CVE-2026-35029** (rated **8.7**, high) is a missing authorisation check on the `/config/update` endpoint: an authenticated user with only read-only access could reach administrative functions, read arbitrary files from the server, **overwrite the admin credentials** by setting environment variables, and achieve **remote code execution** by registering a pass-through endpoint that loads attacker-supplied Python from cloud storage. **CVE-2026-59823** (rated **5.3**, medium) is a **server-side request forgery** flaw in the "Clientside LLM Credentials" feature, letting a user redirect gateway requests to a server they control and **capture the AI provider API keys** carried in the gateway's default headers. Both are fixed in **LiteLLM 1.83.9** and later; the researchers advised upgrading, reviewing who actually holds gateway accounts, and **rotating provider API keys** if compromise is suspected. **Why it matters:** the individual bug classes — missing authorisation, path traversal, RCE via dynamic module loading — are unremarkable, and that is the finding: the AI-specific harm comes from **where** they sit. An LLM gateway is a **centralised trust broker** holding every provider credential, routing every request and enforcing access policy, so a single low-privilege account becomes a path to the organisation's whole AI estate, and a key theft becomes someone else's inference bill. The AUNZ read is a supply-chain one: because LiteLLM is often bundled inside internal tools, the asset register that matters is not "which gateways did we deploy", but "which of our platforms ship one".
- **Source:** [Check Point Research — When LLM Gateways Go Wrong: Findings from LiteLLM](https://research.checkpoint.com/2026/when-llm-gateways-go-wrong-findings-from-litellm/)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (the security vendor's own technical disclosure, with CVE identifiers recorded in the NVD and a fixed version shipped)

## Research & evidence

### 1. METR set out the terms on which it will vouch for a lab — and the limits of voluntary access
- **Summary:** On **14 October** **METR**, the independent evaluator that investigated the **OpenAI/Hugging Face** incident, published a set of five pieces setting out **how it works with AI companies** and how it thinks an assessor should investigate specific risks. The central document states the organisation's purpose — to "give the public **accurate, timely, and independent evidence** about AI **loss-of-control risk**", so that people, policymakers and developers can make their own decisions — and commits it to publishing, for every engagement bearing on public understanding of risk, the **agreed scope and duration**, whether **access limitations** affected its conclusions, whether a company **redacted information material to those conclusions**, and **other formal restrictions or informal pressures** it was under. It says it will disclose **staff conflicts of interest** under its own policy, will not accept payment or donations from frontier AI companies, and has signed no government contracts that restrict its transparency. The companion posts propose how an assessor could investigate **control and monitoring of internal agents**, the **alignment of unreleased models**, **evidence of acceleration from the automation of AI R&D**, and how to **better inform the public about loss-of-control risks**. The candid part is the limitation METR names against itself: it remains **dependent on the companies to engage voluntarily, provide access and permit publication**, sometimes relies on information it cannot verify and which "might be filtered adversarially", and concedes that a company can use its rights to withhold or redact to **exert control over what evidence the public receives**. **Why it matters:** this is the **evidence layer writing down its own terms of engagement** — the closest thing AI has to the independent measurement function a regulator would otherwise perform, and an implicit argument that the current **voluntary** regime cannot deliver assurance at all. It is also a claim to be assessed on METR's record rather than its principles: the commitments are unilateral and unenforceable, and the honest reading is that they make the *limits* of a voluntary evaluation legible rather than removing them. For an AUNZ reader weighing the **Australian AI Safety Institute's** role, the piece is directly on point — it is a worked example of what an evaluator can and cannot promise when its access is a matter of the developer's consent.
- **Source:** [METR — Our approach to working with AI companies](https://metr.org/blog/2026-10-14-our-approach-to-risk-assessment/)

### 2. Mistral released a family of tiny time-series models that run on a CPU
- **Summary:** On **15 October** **Mistral** released **Timestral**, its first family of **time-series foundation models**, in four sizes from **5 million to 209 million parameters** and under a **modified MIT licence**. The largest model ranks first among pretrained, zero-shot entries on all five metrics across the field's three public forecasting benchmarks — **GIFT-Eval**, **TIME** and **fev-bench** — using about two-thirds the parameters of the previous state of the art, according to Mistral, with results submitted to the leaderboards. Timestral produces **probabilistic, multivariate** forecasts (21 quantiles) and supports **past-only and known-future covariates** such as production schedules or weather; it needs **no GPU at inference**, with a standard 8-thread server CPU forecasting one day ahead from three weeks of 15-minute history in **10 milliseconds** on the 5M model and **140 milliseconds** on the 209M model. Mistral says most of the training data was **synthetic**, and offers fine-tuning and deployment **on-premises, in a virtual private cloud or air-gapped** through **Mistral Forge**, with a GitHub repository published for local use. **Why it matters:** the benchmark rankings and latency numbers are **Mistral's own** and the technical report is still to follow, so treat the "state of the art" claim as **a vendor claim** pending independent runs. The interesting part is the shape of the release rather than its size: it is a **small, cheap, air-gappable** model aimed at industrial telemetry, capacity planning and maintenance — the opposite end of the market from the 800-billion-parameter open weights Meta shipped the same day, and a reminder that most enterprise AI value is being delivered by models that never touch a frontier. For an AUNZ reader in energy, resources or logistics, a modifier-MIT forecasting model that runs on a CPU on-premises is a materially different proposition from a cloud-hosted frontier model, on both cost and data-residency grounds.
