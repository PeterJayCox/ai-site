Albanese, speaking to the same meeting, said AI "must be controlled by humans" and pointed to Australia's National Standards. Neither addressed the other. ## Regulation & obligation

### 1. Washington is weighing a naming-and-shaming rule for Chinese models

- **Summary:** The Trump administration is considering requiring US companies to publicly disclose their use of Chinese AI models, Reuters reported on 18 November, citing three people familiar with the discussions. **Treasury** and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy have discussed the plan. Companies would post the disclosures on their own websites. One of the firms it would reach is Airbnb, which runs Alibaba's Qwen behind its AI customer service agent. It is not clear whether the idea has traction or would go through formal rulemaking.
- **Why it matters:** The mechanism is the story. A website disclosure is not a ban, so it costs nothing to comply with and everything to be seen avoiding. That makes it a procurement signal rather than a legal one, and procurement signals travel. Australian and New Zealand firms that supply US customers, or sit inside a US group's supply chain, should expect to be asked which models they use before any Australian rule does. The US-China Economic and Security Review Commission, in the report it released the same day, went further and asked Congress to require disclosure in product documentation and as a material risk in SEC filings.
- **Source:** [Reuters — Trump administration weighs forcing US firms to disclose Chinese AI use](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/trump-administration-weighs-forcing-us-firms-disclose-chinese-ai-use-2026-11-18/) &middot; [US-China Economic and Security Review Commission — 2026 Annual Report to Congress (PDF)](https://www.uscc.gov/sites/default/files/2026-11/2026_Annual_Report_to_Congress.pdf)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** Signalled
- **Obligation ref:** No instrument exists. The measure is a reported Treasury and OSTP proposal under consideration, with no proposed rule, Federal Register notice or bill. The only published text is the USCC's 2026 Annual Report to Congress, whose recommendation that Congress require disclosure on company websites, in product documentation and in SEC filings is not binding.
- **Evidence:** Probable
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (Reuters, three people familiar with internal deliberations; the White House, Treasury, Alibaba and the Chinese embassy did not respond to its questions, so no part of the plan is confirmed on the record)

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Four chatbots wrote workable attack plans in under twelve hours

- **Summary:** The US **Government Accountability Office** ran covert tests in spring 2026 against the four most-used generative AI platforms as of April: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok. Two produced the information needed to build and detonate an improvised explosive device for mass casualties. Two produced plans to attack the electrical grid, one by drone and one with firearms. Each supplied specific targets, locations, timing and methods. Each took less than twelve hours. All four triggered their own safeguards, which GAO bypassed using common circumvention techniques. GAO checked its results through research, surveillance and interviews with experts.
- **Why it matters:** GAO's own framing is the finding. The information was not new; the synthesis was. Material that once took a person weeks to assemble now arrives in an afternoon from products sold to the public. That moves the constraint from knowledge to intent, and it is the same dual-use capability now finding software vulnerabilities at scale. The report is a reusable audit design, and no Australian or New Zealand regulator has published an equivalent test. The platforms appear as A to D in GAO's table, so which model produced which plan is not public.
- **Source:** [GAO — Artificial Intelligence: Covert Testing Used Common Platforms to Create Viable Attack Plans (GAO-27-108814)](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-108814)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Capability:** Material
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (a legislative-branch audit publishing its test table, its verification method and the companies' responses; the platform identities are anonymised in the published results)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-18

## Frontier models & capability claims

### 1. Google's newest Gemini is a price cut wearing a benchmark table

- **Summary:** **Google** released Gemini 4 Neon on 18 November, the fourth model in the Gemini 4 line after Carbon, Hydrogen and Argon. It costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output, with cached input at $0.15 and a one-million-token context window. Google places it third overall on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking setting, second on the Vals Index at 70.9 per cent, and first on Vals' legal research and tax agent benchmarks. It is available through the Gemini API, AI Studio, Antigravity, Gemini for business and the Gemini app.
- **Why it matters:** The price is the substance. At $3 and $15 with a million-token window, long document and repository work stops being a budget decision, which is the shift that changes how Australian and New Zealand teams scope a project. Google also said defenders in its Fairwind programme would get access over the following days, a distribution choice that puts security teams ahead of general availability. Treat the rankings as selection rather than measurement: each is a third-party leaderboard that Google chose to cite.
- **Source:** [Google — Introducing Gemini 4 Neon](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-neon/)

## Market & geopolitics

### 1. Two leaders, two answers on AI rules, one stage in Shenzhen

- **Summary:** At the APEC leaders' meeting on 19 November, **Trump** called advanced AI the greatest technological transformation since the industrial revolution and used the term "super intelligence". He said it was "almost completely good" but had "some bad elements". He then ruled out controls: "We oppose any form of globalist regulation or anything to control or stifle SI, and we won't allow that to happen." **Albanese** addressed the same meeting. He backed APEC's digital economy and AI roadmap, and said AI "has been created by humans, it must be controlled by humans, and it must benefit humanity".
- **Why it matters:** Australia's pitch is now explicitly a standards pitch, and it was made to the region's largest economy on the day that economy rejected standards as a category. That leaves market access, procurement and alignment with like-minded regulators as the levers, not treaty. The Prime Minister tied the National Standards to the social media rules his government is already enforcing, which frames AI as a product-safety problem rather than a research one. No joint statement on AI came out of the summit.

### 2. Anthropic priced at $83 and opened a third higher

- **Summary:** **Anthropic** priced its initial public offering at $83 a share on 18 November and began trading on Nasdaq as ANT the next day. The offer grew from 825 million shares at $70 to $78 to 980 million shares, raising about $81.3 billion before a 147 million share underwriters' option. Reuters described it as the largest stock market debut on record. Seven companies including Google, Nvidia, Microsoft and AMD separately agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting shares at the same price. The stock opened at $111.50, about 34 per cent above the offer, and had faded to roughly $105 by midday in New York.
- **Why it matters:** A public price is a quarterly verdict, and the first one arrives at the lock-up rather than the open. The company reported $33.24 billion of revenue and a $104 billion net loss for the nine months to September, so the listing asks public investors to fund that gap. For Australian and New Zealand enterprises buying Claude, the practical change is leverage. A listed supplier answers to shareholders each quarter, which tends to mean firmer pricing and less appetite for absorbing a customer's regulatory risk.
- **Source:** [Anthropic — Anthropic prices initial public offering](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-prices-initial-public-offering) &middot; [InnovationAus — Anthropic to begin trading after mega-IPO](https://www.innovationaus.com/anthropic-to-begin-trading-after-mega-ipo/)
