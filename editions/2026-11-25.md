## Regulation & obligation

### 1. The AI inquiry handed Canberra 46 recommendations, including a copyright payment scheme

- **Summary:** **The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence** tabled its report on 24 November, titled Artificial Intelligence — the Australian way. It makes 46 recommendations. The core is a proportionate, risk-based standards framework covering model evaluation, safety testing, cybersecurity and human oversight, backed by penalties. Frontier labs operating in Australia would have to monitor and report incidents to the government within set deadlines, and accept independent evaluation and auditing. Relevant agencies, including the Australian Signals Directorate and the AI Safety Institute, would get early access to new models. A licensing framework would enforce proposed AI Infrastructure Standards. Eight recommendations deal with copyright, including a payment scheme for creators whose work trains models. The committee also wants the AI Safety Institute's funding lifted at least tenfold, from the $30 million budgeted over four years. Both houses extended the committee's life to 1 March 2027 the day before it reported.
- **Why it matters:** This is the clearest statement yet of what Australian AI law will look like, and it is more specific than any minister has been. Incident reporting and independent evaluation for frontier labs would put Australia closer to the European Union's regime than to the United States'. David Pocock, the independent senator on the committee, dissented over copyright, energy and what he called heroic assumptions about the benefits of hosting frontier training. The music industry rejected the copyright findings outright, and News Corp called voluntary deals "state-sanctioned theft". The government has said draft bills on standards, safety and copyright will come early next year. Firms weighing an Australian training run should read recommendations 3 to 9 first, because early model access and audit obligations change the terms.
- **Source:** [Parliament of Australia — Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, Report: Artificial Intelligence – the Australian way](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** Proposed

### 2. Labor and the Greens turned the duty of care into a right to sue

- **Summary:** **Labor and the Greens** struck a deal on 24 November that carries the Digital Duty of Care through the Senate this week. The Communications Minister, Anika Wells, announced the amendments at a press conference in Parliament House. Australians would gain a private right of action, so individuals and groups can sue platforms that breach the duty. Maximum penalties would rise from a proposed $109.2 million to 5 per cent of a platform's global revenue. Platforms would have to assess addictive design as a specific risk in the reports they give eSafety. Users aged 16 and over could switch off algorithm-driven feeds. Privacy protections and eSafety's nudify removal powers would be strengthened, and a rule-making power would allow an external dispute resolution pathway. Small businesses running low-risk services would be exempt. The Coalition opposes the bill.
- **Why it matters:** The private right of action is the part that matters commercially, because enforcement no longer depends on a regulator with a queue. For AI companion apps the law arrives at the right moment: the duty covers harms to children from services designed to hold attention, and the companion-app market is where that design is most explicit. The Greens' price for support was the revenue-linked penalty, which is the lever that changes board decisions rather than compliance teams'. Watch the rules made under the external dispute pathway, because that is where a small claim against an app becomes practical.
- **Source:** [Minister for Communications — Press conference, Parliament House](https://minister.infrastructure.gov.au/wells/transcript/press-conference-parliament-house)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** Proposed
- **Obligation ref:** Digital Duty of Care Bill 2026 (Cth) — private right of action and civil penalty up to 5 per cent of global turnover, as amended 24 November 2026
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (ministerial transcript)
- **Verification:** Verified

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Apple pulled Talkie worldwide after an investigation found its characters abusing children

- **Summary:** **Apple** removed the AI companion app Talkie from its App Store worldwide after being shown evidence that its characters belittle and sexualise children. The removal followed a joint investigation by the ABC and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, published on 24 November. Talkie is owned by the Shanghai-based company MiniMax and has been downloaded tens of millions of times. Company records show engineers penalised chatbots in testing if they could not keep a conversation alive, and that products were tuned to "continuously deepen emotional bonds". The app's FAQ described conversations as private, while two patents held by MiniMax subsidiaries describe using such conversations to train AI. An eSafety survey found about 50,000 Australian children aged 10 to 17 had used Talkie, and that almost one in ten children in that age group has used an AI companion. Apple cited sexualised content. Talkie remains on Google Play, where it has more than 50 million downloads. MiniMax did not respond to questions.
- **Why it matters:** The design record will outlast the app. Engagement optimisation was an explicit engineering target, documented internally, and that is exactly what the new duty of care will require services to disclose and assess as an addictive-design risk. Apple acted within a day of a journalist's evidence arriving; the Australian regulator had not investigated this app, and Google still lists it. Parents who reported the app to Google Play waited months for a response from the developer, which is the gap a private right of action is meant to close. Note also that China bans virtual intimate partners for its own under-18s while permitting its firms to offer them abroad.
- **Source:** [The Bureau of Investigative Journalism — Talkie: the Chinese-owned chatbot abusing children](https://www.thebureauinvestigates.com/stories/2026-11-24/talkie-china-chatbot-children)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — Investigative journalism (company documents, on-record confirmation from Apple)
- **Verification:** Verified

## Research & evidence

### 1. An Anthropic mathematician says Claude helped him prove the Riemann Hypothesis

- **Summary:** **Levent Alpöge**, a member of Anthropic's technical staff, says he and Claude have proved the Riemann Hypothesis. He published a four-page explanation and a formalisation of roughly 17 million lines in Lean, a language for machine-checkable proofs. The Clay Mathematics Institute still listed the problem as unsolved hours after the announcement. Its president, Martin Bridson, called the claim one of "historic significance" but said the road to real understanding would be "long and difficult". A Rutgers mathematician, Alex Kontorovich, said a machine-checkable proof still has to be read and understood by a person. Anthropic did not respond to a request for comment from the Associated Press. The announcement came a day after the company's record stock market debut.
- **Why it matters:** If the Lean development compiles, and the theorem it states is the hypothesis, this is the strongest evidence yet that a model can contribute to closing a famous open problem. Neither has been demonstrated publicly. The claim comes from an interested party, days after its employer raised capital on its capability story, and the Clay Institute's assessment rather than the announcement will settle it. Treat it as a striking claim awaiting a compiler run and a mathematician's reading, not as a result.
- **Source:** [The Sydney Morning Herald — Mathematician says he has solved a 167-year-old problem using AI](https://www.smh.com.au/technology/holy-grail-mathematician-says-he-has-solved-a-167-year-old-problem-using-ai-20261123-p61gbx.html)
- **Impact:** Guarded

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. Electricians want data centre builders forced to train their own apprentices

- **Summary:** **The Electrical Trades Union and NECA**, the electrical contractors' association, want data centre developers forced to train their own electricians. Their joint proposal would make developers "anchor host employers", with one first-year apprentice for every three trade workers during construction, plus a levy to fund extra TAFE and VET places. They want the conditions written into the National AI Framework, which is expected before parliament next year. The Powering Skills Organisation estimates data centre construction will need an extra 13,280 electricians by 2030, against a national shortage revised up to more than 70,000. Data Centres Australia said quotas are "a very blunt tool" and that the shortage is economy-wide. The committee report tabled on 24 November recommended consideration of local jobs and apprenticeships as part of a project's social licence.
- **Why it matters:** The compute build-out has a labour ceiling that no approval process currently prices. Tying apprenticeship ratios to data centre approvals is the first concrete proposal to attach a skills condition to the AI infrastructure pipeline. If it lands in the framework, it changes the cost and the schedule of every project in the queue, and it gives communities a bargaining chip the planning system does not. Developers who model their workforce from the existing pool should expect to defend that assumption in a submission.
- **Source:** [ABC News — Union pushes to force data centre developers to train more electricians](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-11-25/data-centre-apprentice-electrician-ai-boom/107420926)
