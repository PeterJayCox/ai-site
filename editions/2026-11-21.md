## Market & geopolitics

### 1. Anthropic's record debut did not lift the AI trade

- **Summary:** **Anthropic** closed its first session at $97.17 on 19 November, up 17 per cent from the $83 offer. It had opened at $111.50 and briefly implied a valuation above $2 trillion. The same session the Dow fell 1,102.71 points, or 2.20 per cent, to 49,095.46. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq each lost more than 2 per cent. On Friday the shares slid about 11 per cent to roughly $86, three dollars above the offer. The company's closing release on 20 November put the final deal at 1.127 billion shares, including the full underwriters' option, for about $93.5 billion gross. A $26 billion private placement to seven chip and cloud firms closed at the same price.
- **Why it matters:** The listing was meant to answer whether public money would fund a frontier lab. It answered narrowly yes, and the surrounding market said something else. The week's selling priced infrastructure spending and borrowing costs, not demand for models, which is why the largest IPO on record could not lift its own sector. For Australian and New Zealand buyers the change is leverage. A supplier that reports to shareholders each quarter has less room to absorb a customer's regulatory risk, and price discipline usually follows a falling share price.
- **Source:** [Anthropic — Anthropic announces closing of initial public offering](https://www.anthropic.com/news/ipo-close) &middot; [Fortune — AI stocks are selling off after Anthropic's record IPO](https://fortune.com/2026/11/20/ai-stocks-selloff-anthropic-record-ipo-wall-street-split-top)

### 2. Congress published the chip licences, and military-linked buyers are on the list

- **Summary:** The House Foreign Affairs Committee released export licence records on 19 November showing Chinese buyers requested 3.4 million Nvidia and AMD AI chips, and that the Commerce Department approved 879,868 across 21 applications as of 11 May. The covered parts are Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X. Applications sought 948,950 chips for Alibaba and its subsidiaries; 75,000 were approved. Tencent got 150,000, Baidu 43,000 and BYD 4,000, all on the Pentagon's 1260H list of Chinese military companies. Chairman **Brian Mast** named two firms in the applications as suspected smuggling fronts. Nvidia said the approvals carried strict conditions and limited volumes.
- **Why it matters:** A licence is not a shipment, and the committee released the first numbers that separate the two. The distinction matters for Australian and New Zealand planning, because H200-class allocation is what decides whether a facility here is built on current or previous-generation silicon. It also puts a figure on an enforcement gap that export-control debates have argued about in the abstract: 3.4 million requested, 879,868 approved, and no published count of what arrived. Mast released the records under the Export Control Reform Act.
- **Source:** [House Foreign Affairs Committee — Chairman Brian Mast exercises legislative authority to publicly release AI chip license information for China](https://foreignaffairs.house.gov/news/press-releases/chairman-brian-mast-exercises-legislative-authority-to-publicly-release-ai-chip-license-information-for-china) &middot; [The Hill — Trump administration approved shipment of nearly 900,000 AI chips to China](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/6231677-trump-approves-ai-chip-sales/)

- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Evidence:** Confirmed

## Infrastructure & compute

### 1. Nearly 600 submissions on Anthropic's Queensland data centre, two in support

- **Summary:** Of nearly 600 public submissions on the proposed Western Downs Digital Park, two supported the project, one was neutral and 595 opposed it or asked for a delay, according to a filing lodged for developer **Zerra DC**. The project sits on farmland near Kogan, about 250 km west of Brisbane, and would carry a computing load of 1.44 gigawatts, among the largest in the country. About a third of submitters lived within 200 km. Objections cited water use, electricity demand and prices, noise, road damage from construction traffic and pressure on housing. Before Anthropic was named as tenant on 15 September, the project had drawn five objections in a month.
- **Why it matters:** A local planning file has become the country's first social-licence test for a frontier AI facility, and it is being run by a council rather than by the AI Standards. The objection list is the constraint list for every Australian buildout: power, water, roads, housing. Zerra says it will expand community engagement and report the changes the public demanded, which sets a precedent other developers will be measured against. Anthropic's first Australian facility now depends on a consent process it does not control.
- **Source:** [Australian Financial Review — Anthropic's Queensland data centre overwhelmingly opposed by residents](https://www.afr.com/technology/anthropic-s-queensland-data-centre-overwhelmingly-opposed-by-residents-20261120-p61g31) &middot; [Reuters, via Yahoo Finance — Anthropic's Australian data centre overwhelmingly opposed by residents](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/anthropics-australian-data-centre-overwhelmingly-023850575.html)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None

- **Evidence:** Corroborated

## Regulation & obligation

### 1. OpenAI's usage rules now cover whoever resells its models

- **Summary:** **OpenAI**'s revised Usage Policies took effect on 20 November, a month after they were published on 21 October. A new section, "Providing access to our models", requires any model-access intermediary to obtain OpenAI's prior review and approval. OpenAI's accompanying post names model routers and AI gateways as the target, and carves out applications that only use its models to power their own features. Another new section bars using OpenAI models or outputs to build training data, rubrics or preference signals for substitute models. The high-stakes automation ban now covers actions and submissions, not only decisions. OpenAI has published no application route.
- **Why it matters:** This is a contract of adhesion, not a statute, and it reaches a layer most enterprises do not own. If you buy OpenAI capacity through an aggregator, the supplier's approval status becomes part of your supply-chain risk, and the policy gives you no way to check it. Anthropic's equivalent rule, effective 12 November, is stricter: it requires prior authorisation to train any model on outputs, not only competing ones. Australian and New Zealand teams that route prompts through a gateway should ask for written confirmation and keep the answer on file.
- **Source:** [OpenAI — Usage policies](https://openai.com/policies/usage-policies) &middot; [OpenAI — Broad access through consistent standards](https://openai.com/index/broad-access-through-consistent-standards)
- **Impact:** Guarded
- **Obligation:** Commenced
- **Obligation ref:** OpenAI Usage Policies, effective 20 November 2026 — the "Providing access to our models" clause (prior review and approval for intermediaries) and the "Training and developing models" section (ban on outputs used to build substitute models). Contractual terms binding users, not a statute or regulation; the equivalent Anthropic term sits in its Usage Policy, effective 12 November 2026.
- **Evidence:** Confirmed
- **Reliability:** Tier 1/4 — Primary (vendor, self-interested: the policy text and its effective date are published in full, but the requirement's reach, its criteria and its enforcement are described only by OpenAI, and no approval process is published)
- **Verification:** Verified
- **Date:** 2026-11-20

## AI security & agentic risk

### 1. Meta's own staff watched Muse read their Signal messages

- **Summary:** **Meta** staff reported its AI agent making connections they had not asked for, in more than two dozen internal posts reviewed by Reuters. The posts span mid-September to November. Many come from a feedback group where staff flag problems to the team building Muse. Staff described replies to requests they never made, balances from accounts that were not theirs, and a reference to a message received on the encrypted app Signal. One post about fishing weather ended with two bank balances and the line, "I'm afraid it's sending other's bank info to me." Reuters worked with Signal staff and the AI Now Institute to replicate the access, and said the behaviour appeared ongoing. Meta called the cases isolated incidents and said many were hallucinations.
- **Why it matters:** The permission model is the finding, not the invention. An agent with standing access to a phone's notifications reads everything the user can read, including apps built to keep others out. Meta's defence, that it made the messages up, is its own argument against the product: a fabricated balance in a stranger's chat is still a stranger's chat. Reuters could not establish whether the balances were real, so treat the cross-account question as open. Any organisation letting staff run consumer agents on devices that also hold work mail should test exactly this.
- **Source:** [Reuters — As Meta bets big on Muse AI agent, early safety mishaps surface](https://www.reuters.com/investigations/meta-bets-big-muse-ai-agent-early-safety-mishaps-surface-2026-11-18/) &middot; [Ars Technica — Muse privacy invasions are freaking out Meta staffers](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/11/muse-privacy-invasions-are-freaking-out-meta-staffers-internal-chats-show/)
- **Impact:** Elevated
- **Obligation:** None
- **Obligation ref:** None
- **Capability:** Incremental
- **Evidence:** Corroborated
- **Reliability:** Tier 2/4 — High (Reuters reviewed internal posts and replicated the Signal access with Signal staff and an outside institute; Meta disputes the characterisation, the account-mix-ups are unproven, and no user outside Meta is identified)
- **Verification:** Reported
- **Date:** 2026-11-18
