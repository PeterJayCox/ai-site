Australian authors and publishers told the AI committee to protect copyright, not water it down

On 21 October the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, chaired by Labor MP Jo Briskey, heard the book industry's case in Sydney: authors Anna Funder, Charlotte Wood and Markus Zusak, Australian Society of Authors chief executive Lucy Hayward, Australian Publishers Association chief executive Patrizia Di Biase-Dyson and head of policy Stuart Glover, and Hachette Australia chief executive Louise Stark. The group called on the Government to protect authors' and illustrators' control over their work and to put consent, transparency and fair payment at the centre of its response to AI and copyright, opposing any weakening of existing law in favour of AI developers. Wood, whose Stone Yard Devotional was shortlisted for the Booker Prize, told the committee that Australia "has excellent copyright laws, which need protection and strengthening", arguing that licensing — through publishers and collecting agencies — already works and is "careful work, but it is not complicated". The committee is examining how AI interacts with copyright, including the use of Australian creative, cultural and media content to train models, and reports by 30 November 2026. Why it matters: it puts the creators' case on the record in person, at the same hearing cycle in which the labs have been pressing theirs — the domestic edge of the training-data question being litigated in the US — and it arrives with the parallel copyright hearings of 16 and 21 October timed before the committee's report shapes any AI-specific legislation. No bill has been introduced; the obligation is proposed.

What it is

A sibling to the Cyber Digest, not a section of it. Where the cyber daily slices the day by industry sector, this one slices it by layer — because AI news has no natural industry axis. Every item is rated on independent axes: how far the risk or obligation position moves, whether it binds an AUNZ organisation, how much the capability itself moved, and how well established the claim actually is.

  • Frontier models & capability claimsModels, agents, releases and the claims attached to them
  • Regulation & obligationInstruments, regulators, enforcement, liability, parliamentary process
  • Research & evidencePapers, evaluations, replication, and the integrity of the research pipeline
  • AI security & agentic riskAgent conduct, exploitation in the wild, AI-component vulnerabilities
  • Infrastructure & computeData centres, chips, energy and water, sovereign capacity
  • Market & geopoliticsState competition, policy divergence, funding, monetisation, adoption

How to read it

Each card ends in a single-line readout: source, then one pill per axis, the source tier, the verification gate and the date. Colour on it means one thing — impact severity — and every other axis is monochrome, so a hue never has to mean two things at once.
