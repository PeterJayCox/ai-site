Australia's AI committee sent the Prime Minister a bipartisan letter — and the Prime Minister's office helped word it

On Tuesday 13 October, Guardian Australia reported that a senior adviser in Anthony Albanese's office made multiple edits to a bipartisan letter from the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence and wrote "fine" on sections, apparently approving lines before the committee sent it to the Prime Minister on the night of 12 October. Notes on the letter show edits as late as 8:55pm, 35 minutes before committee chair Jo Briskey (Labor) and deputy chair Jonno Duniam (Liberal) sent it on behalf of Labor, Coalition and crossbench members; independent senator David Pocock, also on the committee, was asked for edits and provided them at 9:55pm, not knowing the letter had already gone. The letter raises concerns about copyright, AI safeguards, sovereign capability and AI literacy, and calls for "robust safeguards to protect Australian data", changes to copyright law and more investment in data centres. Briskey rejected criticism that the committee's work was "pre-determined", confirming the committee had consulted both Albanese's and the opposition leader's offices before sending; Duniam said there was "no guidance, no interference". Pocock said the markups showed the recommendations were "pre-vetted by an adviser in the prime minister's office", that there was no time for his changes to be considered, and that the letter "doesn't capture the breadth of evidence presented to the committee to date". Why it matters: the letter is not the instrument — it is a signal of direction ahead of the committee's report, due 30 November 2026, and it is the point at which the committee's work becomes a political document. The AUNZ read is that the government is steering the wording of a parliamentary committee's output while legislating nothing, and the dissent is on the record from a crossbench member of that committee. Note the provenance: the underlying letter is not public, so this account rests on documents Guardian Australia says it has seen plus named on-record responses — it is reported from markups, not a published instrument.

What it is

A sibling to the Cyber Digest, not a section of it. Where the cyber daily slices the day by industry sector, this one slices it by layer — because AI news has no natural industry axis. Every item is rated on independent axes: how much the risk or obligation position moves, whether it binds an AUNZ organisation, how much the capability itself moved, and how well established the claim actually is.

  • Frontier capabilityModels, agents and the claims attached to them
  • Research & evidencePapers, evaluations, replication, and the integrity of the research pipeline
  • Regulation & obligationInstruments, regulators, enforcement, liability
  • AI security & agentic riskAgent conduct, exploitation, AI-component vulnerabilities
  • Infrastructure & computeData centres, chips, energy and water
  • Market, geopolitics & adoptionState competition, policy divergence, funding, deployment
