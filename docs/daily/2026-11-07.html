AI security & agentic risk 2 stories

1

Hundreds of the AI hacking tool behind recent intrusions were left open to anyone

Censys found more than 800 deployments of Artex reachable from the internet without a password. Artex is an open-source penetration-testing tool built from AI agents, released in July. Microsoft says it carries an attack from reconnaissance to data exfiltration with little human input. CrowdStrike tied one Artex instance to the September attacks on South Korean financial firms. Microsoft tied the tool to October's ConnectWise breach. Censys researcher Silas Cutler said many instances belong to authorised testers. But the software does not need to be internet-facing, and an exposed instance also exposes the implants and attack data it holds. Artex has since gone closed-source, though copies of its code still circulate.

The tool that made agentic attacks cheap has become a surface in its own right. That is the control lesson for AUNZ defenders: an agent framework is a privileged workload, not a developer utility. Inventory it, authenticate it, and keep it off the public internet. The governance lesson is quieter. Nobody regulates a penetration-testing agent, and nobody had to, because the exposure came from operators rather than the vendor.

2

OpenAI hired the researchers who broke into it, and gave them its cyber models

OpenAI said on 6 November that it had formed a partnership with Hacktron. The two will choose high-priority areas to test together, focused on how OpenAI's systems interact with third-party services. Hacktron's access to frontier models expands through Daybreak Red. OpenAI said it met the firm through its bug bounty program. Hacktron is a small team that used a rival lab's model to reach ChatGPT and Codex accounts through a single sign-on flaw on OpenAI's community forum. That work was reported in September.

This is the disclosure layer doing its job one step late. An outsider found the exposure first, and the vendor's answer was to buy the capability. The scope is the part worth copying. The risk sat where the model met a third-party service, not inside the model. Any AUNZ organisation wiring agents into SaaS should test that seam first.

Regulation & obligation 1 story

3

A NSW inquiry found the approvals system cannot see the harm data centres do together

NSW's upper-house Public Accountability and Works Committee tabled its data centre report on 5 November. It makes 26 recommendations after 143 submissions and five hearings. It found the planning framework assesses each project on its own, while water, power and traffic effects are cumulative. Members voted 6–1 to remove recommendations for a state and national moratorium. Chair Abigail Boyd, a Greens MLC, called for a moratorium anyway, and said industry and the NSW Government had refused to appear as witnesses. On 6 November Deputy Prime Minister Richard Marles said data centres should not be built in the suburbs, as reports said federal Labor was preparing a ban. A Sydney project involving NextDC and OpenAI is awaiting approval.

Siting is becoming the binding constraint on Australian AI capacity, ahead of chips or capital. A pause on new approvals now has majority support in one house of one parliament, and the federal signals point the same way before any bill exists. AUNZ operators should read a planning approval as the scarce asset in the chain, and a community objection as the most likely cause of delay.

Market & geopolitics 2 stories

4

Enterprise AI spending flattened even as usage kept climbing

Ramp reported that token spend across its corporate card base grew 1.5 per cent across August and September combined. It had grown 42 per cent in July. Usage kept rising over the same period. The top 1 per cent of firms, which were spending close to US$10,000 per employee a month at their summer peak, slipped below it. Median firms spend US$12 to $15 per employee a month. Ramp's lead economist Ara Kharazian said almost all AI spending comes from venture-backed, high-growth companies.

Revenue at the frontier labs has been underwritten by a spend curve that just bent. One payments platform is not the market, and its sample leans towards startups. If the pattern holds, price cuts are already doing work that growth was doing. Anthropic's share sale is valued on the other assumption.

5

An OpenAI survey found Australian students building agents faster than their universities can assess them

OpenAI published on 5 November that UNSW, the University of Sydney, La Trobe and Adelaide University have agreements giving students and staff access to its models. It also released a survey of more than 1,000 Australian university students that it commissioned. Four in five said they use AI several times a week. More than a quarter said they had built an agent or assistant to manage study, work or personal life. Nearly four in five wanted more chances to use advanced AI on real-world projects. The post carries no assessment-integrity data.

Adoption is running ahead of the rules that would make it legible. The fact with consequences is undergraduates building agents, because an agent that acts is harder to attribute than an essay that is written. Read the survey as a vendor's market document. Then plan for the behaviour it describes, because the procurement is already done.

Frontier models & capability claims 1 story

6

A startup put a text-to-speech model in 32 megabytes and ran it in a browser

Underdog released Woof, a text-to-speech model it measures at 31.7 megabytes. The demo is a browser game in which every voice is generated on the device. The company posted it on 5 November. The claim is size and local execution rather than benchmark position. No independent evaluation of output quality has been published.

The cost of capability is falling at the small end as fast as it is rising at the large one. A speech model that ships inside a web page changes the privacy argument, because audio never leaves the device. It also changes the economics for AUNZ developers who cannot buy GPU capacity. Treat the quality claim as unproven until someone measures it.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Evidence — how established is the claim?

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. A vendor testifying about its own model is Verified (they did say it) and simultaneously a Vendor claim (the capability is unreplicated). Both badges render.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
