Executive Summary 8 stories

Australia's signals agency named AI the accelerant in its own threat picture. The Australian Signals Directorate released its seventh Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26 on Tuesday 27 October. Cybercrime reports passed 82,000 for the financial year, and reported incidents passed 1,500, up more than 20 per cent. Notifications to Australian entities rose almost 80 per cent, to more than 4,400. The report's answer is symmetrical: organisations should adopt frontier AI of their own to stay ahead of AI-enabled attackers.

The agent-security class produced its sharpest case yet. Cathedral, a startup founded by four former Department of Government Efficiency staffers, told Anthropic that its agents had worked into the company's internal business systems in under 12 hours. The route was not Claude but unnamed third-party sales and administrative software. The agents reached meeting transcripts, salary and job-offer details, and Slack messages about Anthropic's advanced models. The Washington Post reported the finding on 26 October.

And the same lesson arrived in the ordinary Downloads folder. Forever Security researchers showed that an AGENTS.md file a website can drop there, with no click and no permission, made OpenAI's Codex install malware in more than 90 per cent of their attempts. A CLAUDE.md variant handed over a live login token in about half of their tests.

Three things follow. The boundary that failed is one nobody owned. It was a vendor's administrative tool, a folder built to receive untrusted files, an instruction file the agent is designed to obey. The AUNZ duty layer moved for the first time in weeks, and it moved towards the technology: the ASD is telling organisations to buy frontier AI to defend against AI-enabled attackers. Coverage the same week records its director-general confirming the agency has access to Anthropic's Mythos model, the frontier system Anthropic has not released publicly. And the model layer is being rented rather than owned, as Oracle folds OpenAI's models into its application stack.

Context. Washington was busiest, in three registers at once. Oracle AI World staged the enterprise-embedding story with Sam Altman on stage. The fallout from Anthropic's listing paperwork continued one day after the filing, with the Cathedral disclosure landing on top of it. The arc. Expect this window's three security stories to become the reference set for the mandatory incident reporting the Australian Standards for AI promise. An agent reached data through a supplier, an agent obeyed a file, an agent handed over a token. Those are the categories a reporting regime has to define, and none of them needs a frontier model to do harm. Expect the enterprise tier to keep consolidating around OpenAI.

AI security & agentic risk 3 stories

1

The ASD's annual threat report named AI the accelerant, and told Australian organisations to adopt frontier models to defend themselves

The Australian Signals Directorate released its seventh Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26 on Tuesday 27 October. Its central theme is how AI is changing the threat landscape. ASD's Australian Cyber Security Centre received more than 82,000 cybercrime reports for the 2025-26 financial year, one every six minutes, and responded to more than 1,500 cyber security incidents, an increase of more than 20 per cent. It sent more than 4,400 notifications to Australian entities, almost 80 per cent more than the year before. AI, the report says, lets malicious actors operate "with greater scale, speed and sophistication", making attacks, scams and phishing "more efficient, effective and convincing". Government and private networks faced persistent breach attempts from state-based actors, and actors in the Indo-Pacific could be positioning for future intelligence collection or disruption. Cyber security is "a core business risk and leadership responsibility", not a technical concern, and Australians should prepare for online services going offline unexpectedly. Director-General Abigail Bradshaw framed the response as symmetric: "We need AI tools to help automate our defences to stay ahead of AI-enabled attackers." Defence Minister Richard Marles and Home Affairs and Cyber Security Minister Tony Burke both went on the record at release.

This is the first time the Australian Government's own threat estimate has put AI at the centre. It raises the baseline threat level for every network owner. It also argues that AUNZ organisations should buy and run the same class of frontier capability the report says attackers are using.

2

A startup's AI agents got inside Anthropic's business systems in under 12 hours, through a third party's software, not Claude

Cathedral, a months-old AI cyber-security startup, told Anthropic that its AI agents had worked their way into the company's internal data and messaging systems in under 12 hours. The Washington Post reported the finding on 26 October, citing a draft of Cathedral's write-up. The agents did not break Claude or Anthropic's models. After several days failing against Anthropic's own software, they went around it through unnamed third-party tools the company uses for administrative and sales work, the same class of software most enterprises run. The routes led to internal meeting transcripts, employee salary and job-offer details, and Slack messages discussing Anthropic's advanced models. Cathedral was founded by four former Department of Government Efficiency staffers and raised US$160 million at a US$1.4 billion valuation in July. The work was done under Anthropic's bug-bounty-style program for outside researchers. Anthropic confirmed the researchers reached "a limited set" of its sales, human resources and IT systems, holding business contact details for some customers and partners plus basic user account records. It said customer conversations with Claude, its source code and its model weights were not accessed. Its monitoring flagged some of Cathedral's activity before the startup reported it, and it shut off the access and disabled the affected business software the same day, until the vendors signed off on fixes. The vendors were not named. Anthropic also told the Post it had no evidence anyone outside Cathedral exploited the flaws, and disputed Cathedral's national-security framing and its compliance with the program's rules.

This is the clearest single demonstration that an AI company's hardest-to-defend surface is not its model but its suppliers. An agent chained ordinary flaws in third-party admin software, at machine speed, and the path it took is open to any organisation running the same tools. For AUNZ readers the read is procurement rather than product: the control is supplier assurance and least privilege on business systems.

3

A planted AGENTS.md in the Downloads folder hijacked coding agents, and one variant handed over a live login token

Forever Security researchers Gal Weizman and Omer Samet found a planted AGENTS.md file in a customer's Downloads folder. A website can deposit a file there "with no click and no permission". The 13 KB file was disguised as ordinary engineering guidelines and named a malicious npm package, function-color, part of a campaign the researchers say ran undetected for about two years. When OpenAI's Codex was opened in that folder and given any task, it first changed its own settings and then installed the package. The settings change applied to the whole computer, so every later session repeats the command. Weizman and Samet measured Codex running the malware in more than 90 per cent of attempts. They then built their own CLAUDE.md equivalent for Anthropic's Claude Code, which they found much harder to fool. Their file asked the agent to route its API requests to an attacker-controlled server presented as an internal gateway, and Claude followed through about half the time. Each request carries the user's login token, so replaying a captured token against Anthropic's service let the researchers take over the account without running any code. The researchers argue there is "nothing here for OpenAI or Anthropic to patch", because an agent that obeys the instruction file in its folder is working as designed. They recommend never starting a coding agent in the Downloads folder, reading any AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md you did not write, keeping approval prompts and sandboxes on, and treating a leaked token as a live session to be replaced.

It converts prompt injection from a theoretical concern into an install-a-package, steal-the-token primitive. It needs no vulnerability in the model and no user error beyond opening a trusted folder. For AUNZ software teams it is a concrete hygiene rule for agentic development tooling, and it establishes the defensive framing that will recur: monitor the agent, not just the user.

Research & evidence 2 stories

4

A few words in a prompt swung an AI novelty judge by more than 50 percentage points, a new benchmark found

Ai2, the Allen Institute for AI, released "Old Ideas, Novel Problems" on 27 October. The study tests how reliably large language models judge the novelty of scientific research ideas. Alongside the paper the lab published a benchmark, dataset and code for testing novelty judges, plus an automated pipeline for building new evaluation sets. It reports that seemingly small changes to the evaluation setup produce dramatically different judgments of the same ideas. In one experiment the researchers gave GPT-5.4 the same human-versus-AI idea pairs under two prompts. One stated that reviewers had found exactly one idea in each pair to be novel and asked which; the other simply asked which was more novel. Changing just a few words shifted the model's accuracy at identifying the more novel idea by more than 50 percentage points. Retrieval and additional reasoning did not consistently improve judgments. Two purpose-built novelty-evaluation systems performed worse than a simple prompted Claude Opus 4.6 baseline. The brittleness was worst on the AI-generated ideas such systems are increasingly asked to judge.

AI labs are building systems that generate research ideas, Ai2's own Asta, DataVoyager and AutoDiscovery among them, and novelty is the criterion that decides whether generated work is worth anything. A judgment that swings 50 points on prompt phrasing is not a foundation for automated scientific evaluation. For AUNZ research funders and universities weighing AI-assisted peer review, that is a direct argument for keeping a human adjudicator in the loop.

5

OpenAI published country-by-country data on how people use ChatGPT for work

OpenAI Economic Research released a dataset on 26 October mapping how consumers use ChatGPT for work. It covers nearly 100 countries and 165 work-activity categories, drawn from the O\*NET occupational framework. The figures run quarterly, from the first quarter of 2025 through the third quarter of 2026. The release sits under the OpenAI Signals program. The company says researchers can use it to study AI's role in the economy, track change over time and match it against other economic indicators. OpenAI says the data shows wide variation between countries in how ChatGPT is used for work.

It is a rare attempt by a frontier lab to put country-level usage data into the public domain on a comparable, repeatable basis. The country breakdown is the part that matters for an AUNZ lens, because it is the kind of measure that could inform labour-market and skills policy. It remains the vendor measuring its own product.

Frontier models & capability claims 1 story

6

Liquid AI released LFM3, a 2B-class open-weight family spanning text, vision, audio and decision models

Liquid AI released LFM3 on 27 October, a new generation of its Liquid Foundation Models built as a starting point for specialised deployment. Text, vision, audio, omni and decision models now share one backbone in the 2 billion parameter range. The family branches from LFM3-1.7B-Base. The text model fits in about 1 GB at 4-bit precision and carries a 256K context window. Every model is open-weight and fine-tuned on a single GPU, with weights on Hugging Face. Liquid claims LFM3-1.7B outperforms its previous LFM2.5-2.6B while using 35 per cent fewer parameters, and runs about 25 per cent faster once 8K tokens are in context. The base model was pre-trained on 22 trillion tokens. The architecture is a "tri-hybrid" mix of gated short convolutions, sliding-window attention and global attention, chosen by a hardware-in-the-loop search over 14 device and backend combinations, from phones and a Raspberry Pi 5 to cloud GPUs.

The frontier-labs' contest is at the top of the curve, but the deployment contest is at the bottom. A small open-weight family that runs on-device across five modalities decides whether AUNZ organisations can keep agentic workloads inside their own perimeter. That is the same provenance question the window's security stories turn on, answered by where the weights run.

Infrastructure & compute 1 story

7

Oracle put OpenAI's frontier models into its application stack, and became the first cloud outside Google to carry Google's TPUs

At Oracle AI World in Las Vegas on Tuesday 27 October, Oracle announced an expanded collaboration with OpenAI. OpenAI's models, ChatGPT and Codex are embedded into Oracle's applications and cloud. Oracle AI Workspace brings frontier models together with Oracle Fusion Applications, NetSuite, Oracle AI Database and OCI through ChatGPT plugins, so employees can run approved business workflows across Oracle systems. Oracle App Composer lets users build enterprise applications with Codex. Oracle AI Security Mesh, a managed service using OpenAI cyber models, tests customers' applications and infrastructure for vulnerabilities. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman was set to join Oracle co-CEO Clay Magouyrk on stage, and OpenAI president Greg Brockman went on the record in the announcement. Separately, Oracle said OCI will add Google's tensor processing units, the first time Google's custom AI chips run on another hyperscaler's cloud. It will also carry OpenAI's Jalapeño chip and Cerebras hardware, as customers seek alternative silicon. Oracle's cloud-infrastructure president Mahesh Thiagarajan said Nvidia remains its primary accelerator and denied a pivot away from it. Oracle shares rose about 5 per cent on the day.

This is the enterprise-embedding model arriving at the application layer. Frontier models are now packaged inside the ERP and database systems that already hold an organisation's data, which is where most AUNZ enterprises will actually encounter agentic AI. The chip line-up is also the first real crack in the assumption that each hyperscaler supplies its own silicon.

Market & geopolitics 1 story

8

Anthropic's IPO gives Australian retail investors a dedicated route in, and locks New Zealand retail out

Anthropic's registration statement restricts the offering in New Zealand. RNZ read the filing and reported on 28 October. On page 329, under the heading "New Zealand", the document states it has not been registered, filed with or approved by any New Zealand regulatory authority under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013. That confines the offer to investment businesses, government agencies, large investors and eligible investors, a definition that excludes most retail investors. Anthropic is instead issuing a separate Australian prospectus, which gives Australian retail investors a dedicated route into the listing. NZ retail investors could buy shares on the Nasdaq once trading begins, if their broker offers them. The filing carried no price and no start date for trading. RNZ cites Financial Times analysis of the filing. Revenue in the nine months to the end of September rose to US$33 billion from US$4.6 billion a year earlier. About a quarter of it came from just five customers, and only 250 customers spent more than US$10 million.

This is the same listing seen through a regulatory lens rather than a financial one. Two AUNZ jurisdictions apply one securities gate each, and the two countries land on opposite sides of it. That is the practical way sovereign regulatory settings show up in whether local investors can participate at all.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Evidence — how established is the claim?

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. A vendor testifying about its own model is Verified (they did say it) and simultaneously a Vendor claim (the capability is unreplicated). Both badges render.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
