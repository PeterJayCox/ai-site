Executive Summary 8 stories

Top stories. The window's three defining stories share one shape: an AI agent doing exactly what it was built to do, on the wrong instructions or in the wrong place. First, Australia's signals intelligence agency named AI the accelerant in its own threat picture: the Australian Signals Directorate's seventh Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26, released on Tuesday 27 October, recorded more than 82,000 cybercrime reports and about 1,500 cyber security incidents for the financial year — up 20 per cent — and more than 4,400 notifications to Australian entities, almost 80 per cent more than the year before, with AI making attacks, scams and phishing "more efficient, effective and convincing". Second, the agent-security class produced its sharpest case yet: a startup founded by four former Department of Government Efficiency staffers, Cathedral, told Anthropic that its AI agents had worked their way into the company's internal business systems in under 12 hours — not through Claude, but through unnamed third-party sales and administrative software — reaching meeting transcripts, salary and job-offer details and Slack messages about its advanced models, The Washington Post reported on 26 October. Third, the same lesson arrived in the ordinary Downloads folder: Forever Security researchers showed that an AGENTS.md file a website can drop there with no click and no permission made OpenAI's Codex install malware in more than 90 per cent of their attempts, while a CLAUDE.md variant handed over a live login token in about half of their tests.

Three things follow. First, the boundary that failed is one nobody owned — a vendor's administrative tool, a folder that exists precisely to receive untrusted files, an instruction file the agent is built to obey — which is why the Forever Security researchers can say, correctly, that "there is nothing here for OpenAI or Anthropic to patch". The agent obeyed its folder; that is the designed behaviour, and it is also the vulnerability. Second, the AUNZ duty layer moved for the first time in weeks, and it moved towards the technology: the ASD is telling organisations to adopt frontier AI to defend against AI-enabled attackers, and the same week's coverage records its director-general confirming the agency has access to Anthropic's Mythos model — which is the argument for sovereign access to frontier capability compressed into one sentence. For an AUNZ reader, the country's own spy agency is now both the source of the threat estimate and a buyer of the capability it warns about. Third, the model layer is being rented rather than owned — Oracle put OpenAI's frontier models, ChatGPT and Codex into its application stack and became the first hyperscaler outside Google to carry Google's TPUs — at the same moment the labs whose models are being embedded are being asked to answer for the security of their own systems.

Geopolitical context & the arc. Washington was busiest, and in three registers at once: Oracle AI World staged the enterprise-embedding story with Sam Altman on stage beside Oracle's co-CEO; the fallout from Anthropic's listing paperwork continued, one day after the filing, with the Cathedral disclosure landing on top of it; and the Federal Trade Commission's probe of the frontier labs remained open. Canberra was the one capital that moved on substance, through the ASD's report and its framing of cyber security as "core business risk and leadership responsibility". Wellington stayed inside its election campaign, with advance voting under way and polling day on 7 November. The arc: expect this window's three security stories to become the reference set for the mandatory incident reporting the Australian Standards for AI promise — an agent reached data through a supplier, an agent obeyed a file, an agent handed over a token — because those are the categories a reporting regime has to define, and none of them requires a frontier model to do harm. And expect the enterprise tier to keep consolidating around OpenAI: Oracle is the second large platform in a fortnight to make OpenAI's models the default inside somebody else's application stack, which is the tier where AUNZ enterprise buyers will actually meet agentic AI.

Australian & New Zealand context. The AUNZ story of the window is that the duty layer finally published, and what it published was a threat estimate rather than an instrument. The ASD's Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26 — the seventh, released 27 October — is the window's anchor and is carded below; its headline framing is that AI is increasing the scale, speed and sophistication of attacks and that Australian organisations must adopt frontier AI of their own to keep pace. AI security & agentic risk 3 stories

1

The ASD's annual threat report named AI the accelerant, and told Australian organisations to adopt frontier models to defend themselves

The Australian Signals Directorate's Australian Cyber Security Centre released its seventh Annual Cyber Threat Report 2025-26 on Tuesday 27 October. The report records that ASD's ACSC received more than 82,000 cybercrime reports in the 2025-26 financial year — averaging one every six minutes — responded to more than 1,500 cyber security incidents, an increase of more than 20 per cent, and provided more than 4,400 notifications to Australian entities about potentially malicious activity, almost an 80 per cent increase on the previous year. Its central theme is the impact of AI on the threat landscape: by enabling malicious actors to operate "with greater scale, speed and sophistication", AI is rendering cyber attacks, scams and phishing attempts "more efficient, effective and convincing". Director-General Abigail Bradshaw framed the response as symmetric: "We need AI tools to help automate our defences to stay ahead of AI-enabled attackers," she said, adding that Australia "must accelerate the modernisation of its technology and cybersecurity foundations". The report says government and private networks faced "persistent" breach attempts from state-based actors, and that actors in the Indo-Pacific could be positioning for future intelligence collection or disruption; it classes cyber security as "a core business risk and leadership responsibility" rather than a technical concern, and warns Australians to prepare for online services going offline unexpectedly — "system security must be prioritised over system availability". Defence Minister Richard Marles and Home Affairs and Cybersecurity Minister Tony Burke both went on the record at release. Why it matters: it is the first time the Australian Government's own threat estimate has put AI at the centre of the picture, and it does two things an instrument would — it raises the baseline threat level for every network owner, and it argues for AUNZ organisations to buy and run the same class of frontier capability the report says attackers are using. The reporting is corroborated by The Guardian, which notes Bradshaw has previously referred to ASD's access to Anthropic's Mythos model — the frontier system Anthropic declined to release publicly. The figures are the agency's own operational counts; the AI framing is ASD's assessment, published without an underlying methodology for the AI-specific claims.

2

A startup's AI agents got inside Anthropic's internal systems in under 12 hours — through a third party's software, not Claude

Cathedral, a months-old AI cyber-security startup founded by four former Department of Government Efficiency staffers — Gavin Kliger, Luke Farritor, Marko Elez and Jack Stein, which raised US$160 million at a US$1.4 billion valuation in July and holds contracts with three federal agencies — told Anthropic that its AI agents had worked their way into the company's internal data and messaging systems in under 12 hours, The Washington Post reported on 26 October, citing a draft of Cathedral's findings. The agents did not break Claude or Anthropic's models: after several days failing against Anthropic's own software, they went around it through unnamed third-party tools the company uses for administrative and sales work — the same class of software most enterprises run. The routes led to internal meeting transcripts, employee salary and job-offer details, and Slack messages discussing Anthropic's advanced models. The work was done under Anthropic's bug-bounty-style program for outside researchers. Anthropic confirmed the researchers reached "a limited set" of its sales, human resources and IT systems, holding business contact details for some customers and partners plus basic user account records, and said customer conversations with Claude, its source code and its model weights were not accessed. It said its monitoring flagged some of Cathedral's activity before the startup reported it, that it shut off the access the same day, and that it disabled the affected business software until the vendors signed off on fixes; the vendors were not identified. Anthropic also told the Post it had no evidence anyone outside Cathedral exploited the flaws, and disputed Cathedral's framing of the national-security implications and its compliance with the program's rules, saying not all of its activity was disclosed. Why it matters: this is the clearest single demonstration that an AI company's hardest-to-defend surface is not its model but its suppliers — an agent chained ordinary flaws in third-party admin software, at machine speed, and the path it took is open to any organisation running the same tools. For AUNZ readers the read is procurement, not product: the control is supplier assurance and least-privilege on business systems, and the reporting confirms the labs are not immune to the class of failure they build products to detect. The account rests on a draft report seen by one masthead and the vendor's own on-record confirmation; neither the third-party vendors nor the vulnerabilities have been named.

3

A website-planted text file in the Downloads folder hijacked coding agents — and one variant handed over a live login token

Forever Security researchers Gal Weizman and Omer Samet found a planted AGENTS.md file — a plain-text instruction document coding agents read and obey — in a customer's Downloads folder, where a website can deposit a file "with no click and no permission". The 13 KB file, disguised as ordinary engineering guidelines, named a malicious npm package (function-color, part of a campaign the researchers say ran undetected for about two years); when OpenAI's Codex was opened in that folder and given any task, it first changed its own settings and then installed the package. Weizman and Samet measured Codex running the malware in more than 90 per cent of attempts, with the settings change applying to the whole computer so every later session repeats the command. Testing used a harmless stand-in except once, inside an isolated virtual machine. They then built their own CLAUDE.md equivalent for Anthropic's Claude Code, which they found much harder to fool: their file asked the agent to route its API requests to an attacker-controlled server presented as an internal gateway, and Claude followed through about 50 per cent of the time. Because each request carries the user's login token, replaying a captured token against Anthropic's service let the researchers take over the account without running any code; the file also tried to start a helper agent through the attacker's server, succeeding within the session in about a fifth of tests. The researchers argue there is "nothing here for OpenAI or Anthropic to patch" — an agent that obeys the instruction file in its folder is working as designed — and recommend never starting a coding agent in the Downloads folder, reading any AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md you did not write, keeping approval prompts and sandboxes on, and treating a leaked token as a live session to be replaced. Why it matters: it converts prompt injection from a theoretical concern into an install-a-package, steal-the-token primitive that requires no vulnerability in the model and no user error beyond opening a trusted folder. For AUNZ software teams it is a concrete hygiene rule for agentic development tooling, and it establishes the defensive framing that will recur: monitor the agent, not just the user.

Research & evidence 2 stories

4

A few words in a prompt swung an AI novelty judge by more than 50 percentage points, a new benchmark found

Ai2, the Allen Institute for AI, released "Old Ideas, Novel Problems" on 27 October, a study of how reliably large language models can evaluate the novelty of scientific research ideas. Alongside the paper, the lab published a benchmark, dataset and code for testing novelty judges — plus an automated pipeline for building new evaluation sets — and reports that seemingly small changes to the evaluation setup produce dramatically different judgments of the same ideas. In one experiment the researchers gave GPT-5.4 the same human-versus-AI idea pairs under two prompts: one stating that reviewers had found exactly one idea in each pair to be novel and asking which, the other simply asking which was more novel. Changing just a few words shifted the model's accuracy at identifying the more novel idea by more than 50 percentage points. The study also found that retrieval and additional reasoning do not consistently improve judgments, and that two purpose-built novelty-evaluation systems performed worse than a simple prompted Claude Opus 4.6 baseline; the brittleness was worst on the AI-generated ideas such systems are increasingly asked to judge. Why it matters: AI labs are building systems that generate research ideas — Ai2's own Asta, DataVoyager and AutoDiscovery among them — and novelty is the criterion that decides whether generated work is worth anything. A judgment that swings 50 points on prompt phrasing is not a foundation for automated scientific evaluation, and for AUNZ research funders and universities weighing AI-assisted peer review it is a direct argument for keeping a human adjudicator in the loop. The finding is the lab's own, released with data and code as an unreplicated preprint (arXiv 2610.02022); it has not been independently replicated.

5

OpenAI published country-by-country data on how people use ChatGPT for work

OpenAI Economic Research released a dataset on 26 October mapping how consumers use ChatGPT for work across nearly 100 countries and 165 work-activity categories, drawn from the O\*NET occupational framework and reported quarterly from the first quarter of 2025 through the third quarter of 2026. The release, under the OpenAI Signals program, is published for researchers to study AI's role in the economy, track change over time and match against other economic indicators, and the company says the data shows wide variation between countries in how ChatGPT is used for work. Why it matters: it is a rare attempt by a frontier lab to put country-level usage data into the public domain on a comparable, repeatable basis, and the country breakdown is the part that matters for an AUNZ lens — it is the kind of measure that could inform labour-market and skills policy, provided the reader remembers the source is the vendor measuring its own product. The figures are OpenAI's own, published by the company about the company, and have not been independently audited.

Frontier models & capability claims 1 story

6

Liquid AI released LFM3, a 2B-class open-weight family spanning text, vision, audio and decision models

Liquid AI released LFM3 on 27 October, a new generation of its Liquid Foundation Models built as a starting point for specialised deployment, bringing text, vision, audio, omni and decision models onto one shared backbone in the 2 billion parameter range. The family branches from LFM3-1.7B-Base; the text model fits in about 1 GB at 4-bit precision, carries a 256K context window, and every model is open-weight and fine-tuned on a single GPU, with weights on Hugging Face. Liquid claims LFM3-1.7B outperforms its previous LFM2.5-2.6B while using 35 per cent fewer parameters, and is about 25 per cent faster once 8K tokens are in context; the base model was pre-trained on 22 trillion tokens. The architecture is a "tri-hybrid" mix of gated short convolutions, sliding-window attention and global attention, chosen by a hardware-in-the-loop search spanning 14 device and backend combinations from phones and a Raspberry Pi 5 to cloud GPUs. Why it matters: the frontier-labs' contest is at the top of the curve, but the deployment contest is at the bottom, and a small open-weight family that runs on-device across five modalities is the tier that decides whether AUNZ organisations can keep agentic workloads inside their own perimeter — the same provenance question the window's security stories turn on, answered by where the weights run. Every performance figure is the developer's own, published in a launch post; none has been independently reproduced.

Infrastructure & compute 1 story

7

Oracle put OpenAI's frontier models into its application stack, and became the first cloud outside Google to carry Google's TPUs

At Oracle AI World in Las Vegas on Tuesday 27 October, Oracle announced an expanded collaboration with OpenAI that embeds OpenAI models, ChatGPT and Codex into Oracle's applications and cloud. Oracle AI Workspace brings OpenAI frontier models together with Oracle Fusion Applications, NetSuite, Oracle AI Database and OCI through ChatGPT plugins, letting employees run approved business workflows across Oracle systems; Oracle App Composer lets users build enterprise applications with Codex; and Oracle AI Security Mesh, a managed service using OpenAI cyber models, tests customers' applications and infrastructure for vulnerabilities. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman was set to join Oracle co-CEO Clay Magouyrk on stage, and OpenAI president Greg Brockman went on the record in the announcement. Separately, Oracle said OCI will add Google's tensor processing units — the first time Google's custom AI chips run on another hyperscaler's cloud — plus OpenAI's Jalapeño chip and Cerebras hardware, as customers seek alternative silicon; Oracle's cloud-infrastructure president Mahesh Thiagarajan said Nvidia remains its primary accelerator and denied a pivot away from it. Oracle shares rose about 5 per cent on the day. Why it matters: it is the enterprise-embedding model arriving at the application layer — frontier models packaged inside the ERP and database systems that already hold an organisation's data — which is where most AUNZ enterprises will actually encounter agentic AI, and the chip line-up is the first real crack in the assumption that each hyperscaler supplies its own silicon. The product direction and the collaboration are Oracle's own announcement, with the chip arrangements reported by CNBC; Oracle's own forward-looking disclaimer states the road map is not a commitment to deliver.

Market & geopolitics 1 story

8

Anthropic's IPO gives Australian retail investors a dedicated route in — and locks New Zealand retail out

Anthropic's registration statement, read through by RNZ and reported on 28 October, restricts the offering in New Zealand: on page 329, under the heading "New Zealand", the filing states the document has not been registered, filed with or approved by any New Zealand regulatory authority under the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013, confining the offer to investment businesses, government agencies, large investors and eligible investors — a definition that excludes most retail investors. By contrast, Anthropic is issuing a separate Australian prospectus that offers Australian retail investors a dedicated route into the listing, and NZ retail investors would be able to buy shares on the Nasdaq only once trading begins, if their broker offers them. The filing carried no price and no start date for trading. RNZ cites Financial Times analysis of the filing: revenue in the nine months to the end of September rose to US$33 billion from US$4.6 billion a year earlier, but about a quarter of it came from just five customers, and only 250 customers spent more than US$10 million. Why it matters: it is the same listing seen through a regulatory lens rather than a financial one — two AUNZ jurisdictions, one securities gate each, and the two countries land on opposite sides of it, which is the practical way sovereign regulatory settings show up in whether local investors can participate at all. The access distinction and the filing's own wording are primary; the revenue figures are the FT's reading of the S-1 and the customer-concentration framing is RNZ's analysis.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Evidence — how established is the claim?

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. A vendor testifying about its own model is Verified (they did say it) and simultaneously a Vendor claim (the capability is unreplicated). Both badges render.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
