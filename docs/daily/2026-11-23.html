A model marketplace pulled three providers over logged prompts

OpenRouter off-boarded three inference providers and removed 125 endpoints from its Zero Data Retention eligibility list. The marketplace announced the action on 22 November, after Dragos researchers ran canary-token prompts against hosted models from July to November. Their tests found resellers logging prompts, and providers ignoring no-collection flags. OpenRouter says no breach touched its own systems. Three providers have posted notices: Together, which admitted prompt leakage for requests made in June and July; Novita, on MiniMax requests; and Phala, in a preliminary incident report.

Why it matters

A retention badge on a model marketplace is a promise, and nothing was auditing it. Dragos only found this by planting tokens and watching where they surfaced. Any team sending proprietary code or customer data through a reseller should treat the label as unverified, and check which provider actually serves its endpoint. Australian and New Zealand firms that buy inference through aggregators rather than direct now have a documented failure mode in that chain.

The world agreed to keep talking about autonomous weapons until 2028

The UN talks on lethal autonomous weapons ended in Geneva on 21 November with an agreement to elaborate a possible instrument by 2028. All 128 states party to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons backed the outcome, reached after an all-night session. Around 100 states wanted a mandate for binding rules. The United States, Russia and Israel opposed one, and the text leaves the question of legal force open. The Swiss chair, Julien Thoeni, called it a significant humanitarian achievement. Stop Killer Robots said the delay puts civilians at risk.

Why it matters

A consensus body of 128 states moves at the pace of its least willing member, and three large arms producers are the least willing. Weapons that select and engage targets are already in use in Ukraine, Sudan and the Middle East. So the practical effect of this outcome is to leave targeting decisions to national policy for at least two more years. Defence suppliers and dual-use exporters should read those national rules as the binding constraint, not the UN process.

Regulation & obligation 2 stories

3

Four governors asked Congress to write the frontier-model rules

The National Governors Association published AI principles on 22 November, after work by a bipartisan council. It was led by Maryland's Wes Moore, the association's chair, and Indiana's Mike Braun, its vice chair, with North Carolina's Josh Stein and Utah's Spencer Cox. The document asks Congress to set complementary federal and state roles. Washington would take national and international risks, with independent evaluation of the most advanced models, and incident reports to federal authorities and affected states. States would keep their powers over consumers, discrimination and workforce transition.

Why it matters

The argument is over where the line between federal and state AI law sits, and this plants a marker from both parties. It also leaves the hardest definition open: the principles set no compute or revenue threshold for what counts as a frontier model. Labs would get one set of rules to track. Companies building on top of their models keep facing state law, which is a different settlement from the pre-emption the industry has lobbied for.

4

New Zealand is reviewing copyright for AI-assisted works

RNZ reported on 23 November that New Zealand's Government is reviewing copyright legislation, and that an Auckland scholar wants clearer rules for works made with AI. Joshua Yuvaraj, of Auckland Law School, argues that protection should turn on genuine human contribution. He grounds that in emotion, morality and community, and says a work made with AI assistance can still qualify where a person supplies real skill and labour, including through prompts. He also wants the law to be clear on training data and on compensation for artists.

Why it matters

New Zealand has no settled test for AI-assisted authorship, and a review with no bill in front of it is the cheapest point to influence. The practical advice in the piece is already useful: document what the human contributed. New Zealand is in a caretaker period after the 7 November election, so expect nothing to be enacted until a government forms. Australian creators face the same question under a different statute, and the two regimes have diverged before.

5

OpenAI published its own loss curve and proposed an industry limit

OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, published an argument on 22 November for treating perplexity as the industry's shared measure of progress. The post plots log-perplexity falling from about 0.99 at GPT-3.5 to below 0.8 in current models. It links that drop to capabilities appearing in mathematics and cybersecurity. He proposes that labs commit to shared limits, measured on defined metrics, and not cross them until monitoring has caught up. He concedes that labs use different datasets, so the numbers do not compare directly.

Why it matters

Every figure here is OpenAI's own, measured on an internal dataset it does not publish, so the curve is a claim rather than a measurement anyone can check. The proposal matters more than the plot. A limit needs a metric all labs accept, an agreed threshold and a way to verify compliance, and this post supplies only the first. It is an opening bid in a standards argument, and it argues for pacing on terms the vendor has chosen.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
