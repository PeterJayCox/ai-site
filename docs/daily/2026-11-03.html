1

Anthropic's filing disclosed US$895 billion of commitments against US$33 billion of revenue

Anthropic filed an amended S-1 with the US Securities and Exchange Commission on 2 November. The document reports US$33.24 billion of revenue for the nine months to 30 September, up 1,200 per cent on the year before. Revenue for the September quarter alone was about US$17 billion, according to Bloomberg. The same filing reports a US$104 billion net loss for the nine months, mostly accounting charges, and a US$23.29 billion net loss for the quarter. It also discloses US$895.4 billion of commitments, "mostly on compute and infrastructure".

The commitment figure is the story, because it is far larger than the revenue that has to service it. The listing is a bet that capacity bought now converts into revenue later, and the bet is now public. For an AUNZ buyer, it means the largest vendors are locked into contracts that must be paid whatever demand does. It also gives regulators the first hard number for what a frontier lab owes.

Infrastructure & compute 2 stories

2

Marvell bought the optical plumbing of AI clusters for US$115 billion

Marvell agreed on 2 November to acquire Lumentum in an all-stock deal with an implied enterprise value of US$115 billion. Lumentum holders receive 4.5 Marvell shares each, about US$1,132 a share and a 23.9 per cent premium to the close on 28 October. Marvell holders will own about 67 per cent of the combined company. Both firms make the electrical and optical parts that link accelerators inside and between racks. Marvell said copper is reaching its limits and the industry is moving to integrated optical links. It expects more than US$1 billion in annual cost savings.

Bandwidth, not transistors, is becoming the binding constraint on clusters built at gigawatt scale. Buying the optics supplier is how a chip designer controls that layer, and the price says what the market thinks the layer is worth. For AUNZ buyers the signal is supply concentration: one fewer independent optics vendor, and pricing power moving to the few firms that can co-design both sides.

3

OpenAI promised to turn its campuses down when the grid is short

OpenAI launched a "Grid Protectors" programme on 2 November, promising that future campuses will cut their power draw when the electricity system is under strain. The template is Effingham County in Georgia. OpenAI has agreed with Georgia Power to pay the full infrastructure and service costs for that campus, and to provide up to 1,000 megawatts of flexible demand. The company says that equals the average demand of about 700,000 households. Georgia Power projects about US$950 million in annual customer savings from 2029 across its large-load agreements.

This is the argument Australian regulators and host communities have been making, now made by the developer. It is also positioning: the same company is briefing that its facilities are "AI compute campuses" rather than data centres. Treat the savings figure as the utility's projection, not a measured outcome. The test for any AUNZ copy of this model is whether the flexibility is contracted and enforceable, not announced in a blog post.

Regulation & obligation 2 stories

4

OpenAI will argue for mandatory "speed limits" on the most capable models

OpenAI plans a policy campaign after the US midterms on 3 November, Axios reported on 1 November. Its centrepiece is a call for mandatory safeguards on the most powerful systems, which the company calls "speed limits". Global affairs chief Chris Lehane said the push begins in the lame-duck session that opens on 9 November. OpenAI will also propose state legislation giving communities a say in data-centre approvals, and will argue its facilities should be called "AI compute campuses". No proposal has been drafted or introduced.

A frontier lab asking to be regulated is a strategic move as much as a concession, and it lands as Democrats expect to win the House. The AUNZ read is that the vocabulary will travel: Australia's frontier-AI model, set out in October, rests on standards and tiers rather than a statute. Watch whether Australian officials adopt "speed limits" before any bill exists here.

5

Australian artists turned the copyright fight into a contest in the PM's own seat

Guardian Australia reported on 1 November that artists and studio operators in Grayndler, Anthony Albanese's electorate, are organising against the government's AI copyright plan. The proposal would let AI developers train on Australian material unless rights holders opt out, a design Anthropic put to ministers. Labor has promised "the strongest possible protection" while warning that unpaid use would be "theft". Grayndler holds more than 3,000 cultural and creative businesses, and 10 per cent of its workers are in cultural fields against 4.2 per cent nationally. Draft laws are expected by the end of the year, with parliamentary consideration in 2027.

The objection is not abstract. A safe seat with a tenth of its workforce in cultural work is where a national opt-out model becomes a local electoral problem, and the Prime Minister's own seat is the sharpest place for it to surface. For AUNZ organisations the practical warning is that the default is still being set, so any licence or policy agreed now is written against a rule that may change twice before it settles.

Research & evidence 1 story

6

A new benchmark put the best frontier model at 12 per cent, and blamed the safety filters

SemiAnalysis published GDPx on 2 November, a benchmark spanning 11 domains including chip design, cybersecurity, biology, finance, recursive self-improvement and alignment. The best model, Opus 5.5, scored 12 per cent. The firm says safety classifiers suppressed the scores of Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra in cyber, biology and alignment, and it publishes a second ranking with those three domains removed. It names Muse Wonder Full as the best model available for cyber work today, a result it ties to rivals' filters refusing tasks. Gemini 4 Carbon was the only other model in the same band.

The finding worth keeping is the mechanism, not the ranking. If guardrails decide a model's place on a capability leaderboard, then published scores measure the product's safety settings as much as its reasoning. That cuts both ways for AUNZ buyers: a lower score may mean a safer default, and a vendor's headline number may describe a configuration you cannot buy. One evaluator, a paywalled method and no replication make this a claim to hold lightly.

AI security & agentic risk 1 story

7

More than 400 apps hid sexual deepfake tools behind pet portraits and study aids

WIRED reported on 2 November that more than 400 domains and apps hide interfaces for generating sexual deepfake images and video. The apps present as ordinary services, including pet portraits, language tuition and AI workout coaching. Under some conditions they load a generator whose scenes are named "bedroom rape" and "threatening knife". Researchers traced the network through thousands of video ads on Facebook and Instagram, and payments ran through Apple and Google. Domain records point to Chinese developers and a Hong Kong entity, KeLing Technology. The operator did not respond to WIRED.

This is the platform-enforcement failure the AI-safety debate usually describes in the abstract, with receipts. App review, ad review and payment processing each let it through, for about a year, at dozens of new apps a week. The AUNZ hook is direct: the eSafety Commissioner's deepfake powers and the proposed digital duty of care both assume a platform can be told to find this material. The finding is that discovery, not authority, is the gap.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Evidence — how established is the claim?

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. A vendor testifying about its own model is Verified (they did say it) and simultaneously a Vendor claim (the capability is unreplicated). Both badges render.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
