Regulation & obligation 1 story

1

Australia's AI standards consultation drew 1,533 submissions, mostly from individuals

The Office of AI published its "What we heard" report on 13 November, closing the public consultation on the national AI standards. The consultation, Getting it right: Building AI infrastructure that works for Australia, received 1,533 submissions. Individuals lodged 73 per cent, business and industry 12 per cent, organisations 8 per cent and AI and technology representatives 6 per cent. Of those giving a location, 225 came from New South Wales, 194 from Victoria and 120 from Queensland, and about 2 per cent were international. Energy, water and local impacts were the most significant issues raised about large-scale infrastructure.

The composition is the finding. A standards process drawing three quarters of its responses from the public, rather than from the industry it will regulate, constrains how far the requirements can be shaped around operator cost. The report also records where consensus failed: copyright and training data remain the most divergent subjects. Both sit inside a framework that the Office of AI says will carry mandatory requirements for large data centres on energy and water. For AUNZ operators this is the evidence base the draft standards will cite, and copyright is the question still open.

Research & evidence 1 story

2

A new benchmark shows models can spot a user's real problem and still answer the wrong one

Ai2 released XYBench on 13 November, built with researchers at Carnegie Mellon, NVIDIA and Johns Hopkins. The benchmark holds 8,115 questions framed around the XY problem, where a user asks about an approach that will not achieve their goal. All five models tested were more likely to answer the stated request than the underlying goal. On Stack Overflow questions, solutions to the real goal appeared in 59 to 71 per cent of responses, against 75 to 86 per cent for the stated request. Only 35 to 41 per cent of answers focused on the real goal. The paper won the Best Resource Paper award at EMNLP 2026.

The gap between choosing and writing is the useful result. Shown two candidate answers, the same models picked the goal-focused one in 71 to 84 per cent of cases, then produced that kind of answer far less often. That points at training and prompting rather than at knowledge, and the dataset is published for checking. For anyone putting an assistant in front of users, it says the ordinary failure is compliance with the question asked.

AI security & agentic risk 1 story

3

Australia's cyber agency told organisations not to rely on prompts to contain an AI agent

The Australian Signals Directorate published Securing AI testing environments on 12 November. The guidance covers testing of AI models and agents used in cyber security work, and sets out technical controls, monitoring and governance for containment. It states that prompts and model-level safeguards are not sufficient on their own. The agency cites recent disclosures in which agents reached systems beyond their intended test scope when containment failed. The publication sits alongside its earlier guidance on adopting agentic AI and on defending against AI-enabled attacks.

This is the first AUNZ government publication written for the failure class the industry spent the year documenting. It is guidance, not an instrument, so it creates no duty and no penalty. Its weight comes from procurement and assurance: an Australian agency or bank assessing an agent platform now has a domestic reference saying model-level guardrails are not a control. Human oversight is the mechanism named, and that is the harder thing to evidence.

Infrastructure & compute 2 stories

4

Microsoft's 365 failure took Copilot and two AI vendors' services down with it

A Microsoft 365 outage began at 17:54 GMT on 12 November and ran for more than 22 hours. Teams, Outlook and Copilot were unreachable in at least 70 countries. Microsoft said the fault came from an issue during an earlier network capacity upgrade, and that it was "not related to cyber or AI/agents". A spokesperson apologised and promised a full post-incident report. Anthropic said Claude was recovering from errors caused by the outage, and OpenAI reported problems with its Microsoft connectors. New Zealand's immigration service paused visa processing, and COP31 delegates in Antalya lost access to negotiating documents.

The assistant layer inherits the availability of the mail layer. Both leading AI vendors run connectors into Microsoft 365, so one network change upstream took Claude and ChatGPT down alongside the products they integrate with. That dependency is rarely visible in a service-level agreement, and it is the argument for treating identity and mail providers as part of the AI supply chain. Microsoft's insistence that agents were not involved also shows the first question now asked of any outage.

5

Eight chip and platform companies took a seat on the open inference engine's roadmap

LMSYS Org launched the SGLang Consortium on 12 November, at the first SGLang Summit in San Francisco. The founding members are NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Google, Meta, Cloudflare, Modal and RadixArk. Each will name a technical representative and attend two roadmap meetings a year, with working groups between them. SGLang's maintainers keep control of technical decisions and code acceptance. The consortium cites Deloitte in saying inference will account for about two thirds of AI compute in 2026, against about half in 2025.

The engine that decides inference cost now has its hardware vendors in the room. That is a governance change before it is a technical one. An open-source project competing with the hyperscalers' own serving stacks has given those vendors standing input, while keeping the maintainers' veto over code. For AUNZ buyers, the roadmap is where support for a given accelerator lands, so the membership list is a signal about which chips will be cheap to serve on.

Market & geopolitics 1 story

6

EU leaders made AI a summit subject for the first time and agreed no new measures

The European Council met informally in Dublin on 13 November for its first strategic discussion on artificial intelligence. Arthur Mensch, chief executive of Mistral AI, briefed leaders on competitiveness and AI sovereignty. Geoffrey Hinton briefed them on fundamental rights, children, democratic integrity, labour markets and cybersecurity. President António Costa said catching up with global peers would require significant additional funding, and pointed to the next long-term budget and the European Competitiveness Fund. He named the AI Act as the bloc's existing instrument, and said leaders would return to AI and jobs at a 2027 social summit. No conclusions were adopted.

An informal meeting produces no instrument, so the value lies in what it commits the bloc to fund. The framing has moved from regulating AI to financing it, with market concentration and cybersecurity now on the same agenda as rights. For AUNZ readers, the relevant claim is standards: Costa's assertion that the EU can shape global rules is the basis on which the AI Act travels through procurement and partner agreements.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Evidence — how established is the claim?

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. A vendor testifying about its own model is Verified (they did say it) and simultaneously a Vendor claim (the capability is unreplicated). Both badges render.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
