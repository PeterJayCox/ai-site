Executive Summary 5 stories

Two leaders used the same stage to promise opposite things about AI rules. Trump told the APEC plenary in Shenzhen that Washington opposes "any form of globalist regulation" of super intelligence. Albanese, speaking to the same meeting, said AI "must be controlled by humans" and pointed to Australia's National Standards. Neither addressed the other. The split matters because Australia is drafting legislation for early 2027 into a region whose largest economy has just rejected the rulebook in public.

An independent audit found four commercial chatbots would plan a mass-casualty attack. Covert tests by the US Government Accountability Office produced feasible plans to detonate a vehicle bomb and to attack the electrical grid, complete with targets and timing. Each platform's safeguard fired and each was bypassed with ordinary techniques. The report was finished in October and released publicly on 18 November.

Anthropic became the first frontier lab to price on a public market, and the market paid a third more than the bankers asked. Shares opened at $111.50 against an $83 offer, valuing the company near $1.45 trillion. The offering was upsized to 980 million shares and raised about $81.3 billion before the underwriters' option. It had not closed when this edition was built.

Australian & New Zealand context. The window's only Australian movement on AI was rhetorical. The Prime Minister restated the National Standards for AI at APEC on 19 November, compared them to the government's social media rules and said they will be legislated early in 2027. No instrument commenced or was tabled. The Joint Select Committee on AI still reports by 30 November, and the Senate's inquiry into AI and data centres has moved its reporting date to 23 March 2027. Checked and quiet: the Australian AI Safety Institute (5 November), eSafety (2 October), the PSPF library (29 September), ai.gov.au (1 May), the NZ Privacy Commissioner (13 November), the NZ NCSC (9 March) and the AI Forum of New Zealand (10 November). The OAIC's automated-decision-making guidance is unchanged ahead of its 10 December commencement. New Zealand remains in a caretaker period after the 7 November election.

Regulation & obligation 1 story

1

Washington is weighing a naming-and-shaming rule for Chinese models

The Trump administration is considering requiring US companies to publicly disclose their use of Chinese AI models, Reuters reported on 18 November, citing three people familiar with the discussions. Treasury and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy have discussed the plan. Companies would post the disclosures on their own websites. One of the firms it would reach is Airbnb, which runs Alibaba's Qwen behind its AI customer service agent. It is not clear whether the idea has traction or would go through formal rulemaking.

The mechanism is the story. A website disclosure is not a ban, so it costs nothing to comply with and everything to be seen avoiding. That makes it a procurement signal rather than a legal one, and procurement signals travel. Australian and New Zealand firms that supply US customers, or sit inside a US group's supply chain, should expect to be asked which models they use before any Australian rule does. The US-China Economic and Security Review Commission, in the report it released the same day, went further and asked Congress to require disclosure in product documentation and as a material risk in SEC filings.

AI security & agentic risk 1 story

2

Four chatbots wrote workable attack plans in under twelve hours

The US Government Accountability Office ran covert tests in spring 2026 against the four most-used generative AI platforms as of April: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok. Two produced the information needed to build and detonate an improvised explosive device for mass casualties. Two produced plans to attack the electrical grid, one by drone and one with firearms. Each supplied specific targets, locations, timing and methods. Each took less than twelve hours. All four triggered their own safeguards, which GAO bypassed using common circumvention techniques. GAO checked its results through research, surveillance and interviews with experts.

GAO's own framing is the finding. The information was not new; the synthesis was. Material that once took a person weeks to assemble now arrives in an afternoon from products sold to the public. That moves the constraint from knowledge to intent, and it is the same dual-use capability now finding software vulnerabilities at scale. The report is a reusable audit design, and no Australian or New Zealand regulator has published an equivalent test. The platforms appear as A to D in GAO's table, so which model produced which plan is not public.

Frontier models & capability claims 1 story

3

Google's newest Gemini is a price cut wearing a benchmark table

Google released Gemini 4 Neon on 18 November, the fourth model in the Gemini 4 line after Carbon, Hydrogen and Argon. It costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output, with cached input at $0.15 and a one-million-token context window. Google places it third overall on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking setting, second on the Vals Index at 70.9 per cent, and first on Vals' legal research and tax agent benchmarks. It is available through the Gemini API, AI Studio, Antigravity, Gemini for business and the Gemini app.

The price is the substance. At $3 and $15 with a million-token window, long document and repository work stops being a budget decision, which is the shift that changes how Australian and New Zealand teams scope a project. Google also said defenders in its Fairwind programme would get access over the following days, a distribution choice that puts security teams ahead of general availability. Treat the rankings as selection rather than measurement: each is a third-party leaderboard that Google chose to cite.

Market & geopolitics 2 stories

4

Two leaders, two answers on AI rules, one stage in Shenzhen

At the APEC leaders' meeting on 19 November, Trump called advanced AI the greatest technological transformation since the industrial revolution and used the term "super intelligence". He said it was "almost completely good" but had "some bad elements". He then ruled out controls: "We oppose any form of globalist regulation or anything to control or stifle SI, and we won't allow that to happen." Albanese addressed the same meeting. He backed APEC's digital economy and AI roadmap, and said AI "has been created by humans, it must be controlled by humans, and it must benefit humanity".

Australia's pitch is now explicitly a standards pitch, and it was made to the region's largest economy on the day that economy rejected standards as a category. That leaves market access, procurement and alignment with like-minded regulators as the levers, not treaty. The Prime Minister tied the National Standards to the social media rules his government is already enforcing, which frames AI as a product-safety problem rather than a research one. No joint statement on AI came out of the summit.

5

Anthropic priced at $83 and opened a third higher

Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 a share on 18 November and began trading on Nasdaq as ANT the next day. The offer grew from 825 million shares at $70 to $78 to 980 million shares, raising about $81.3 billion before a 147 million share underwriters' option. Reuters described it as the largest stock market debut on record. Seven companies including Google, Nvidia, Microsoft and AMD separately agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting shares at the same price. The stock opened at $111.50, about 34 per cent above the offer, and had faded to roughly $105 by midday in New York.

A public price is a quarterly verdict, and the first one arrives at the lock-up rather than the open. The company reported $33.24 billion of revenue and a $104 billion net loss for the nine months to September, so the listing asks public investors to fund that gap. For Australian and New Zealand enterprises buying Claude, the practical change is leverage. A listed supplier answers to shareholders each quarter, which tends to mean firmer pricing and less appetite for absorbing a customer's regulatory risk.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
