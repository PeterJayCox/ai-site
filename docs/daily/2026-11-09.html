1

The global music industry told Albanese that an AI copyright carve-out would be a "disaster"

The International Federation of the Phonographic Industry has warned Australia against weakening copyright law to let AI companies train on local material. Its chief executive, Victoria Oakley, told Guardian Australia that a carve-out would be "ridiculous and dangerous". She argued that a single safe harbour would let a model train on music from anywhere in the world. The intervention lands while the government weighs changes AI companies have requested. Anthropic has proposed an opt-out model, where content is scraped unless a rights holder objects. OpenAI told a parliamentary committee that licensing music for streaming is a different business from training on it. The Attorney-General, Michelle Rowland, has said any changes must deliver "meaningful control and fair compensation". Australian labels have raised constitutional concerns about a compensation case.

A safe harbour granted here would cover training that reaches every market, so the cost falls on rights holders who never agreed to it. That is why the lobby is offshore: Australia is being argued about as a precedent, not as a market. The Joint Select Committee on AI reports by 30 November, and copyright is the question it cannot avoid. Read the committee's findings before the minister's next statement, because the committee is where the text will first appear.

Elvis Presley's rights holders and Eminem's publisher sued Suno under Tennessee's voice law

Two groups of music rights holders filed federal lawsuits against the AI music generator Suno in the Northern District of California. The Presley action, brought by Authentic Brands Group, Raleigh Music Publishing and others, runs to 51 pages. It alleges copyright infringement and a breach of Tennessee's Ensuring Likeness Voice and Image Security Act, a 2024 law against cloning a singer's voice. The filing says Suno reproduces Presley's "rich vocal timbre" without permission. Eight Mile Style, which controls much of Eminem's catalogue, filed separately over generated songs and lyrics; Eminem is not a party. Suno said it blocks prompts naming specific artists and screens uploads.

Australia has no likeness right for a voice. A local artist's remedy runs through passing off and section 18 of the Australian Consumer Law, both of which need a misleading representation rather than a cloned timbre. The United States is where the rule is being written, state by state, and the ELVIS Act is the first statute to name the harm. AUNZ labels and studios should assume the licensing market forms offshore first.

3

Congress turned on Nvidia's China business after a spy report and an empty office

Three House Republican committee chairs asked Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, to appear for a closed-door briefing on 18 November, a day after the company reports quarterly earnings. The letter cites national-security concerns about Chinese access to sensitive technology. Senator Chris Coons, in line to chair Foreign Relations in January, said Congress would investigate. The reports behind it: The New York Times said a memo identified Megaspeed executive Alice Huang as recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. Nvidia says Huang's contacts with her were ordinary customer meetings. Separately, Senator John Cornyn publicised a Times report that Commerce approved New Matrix to assemble Nvidia chips in Beijing despite little evidence the firm operates. Its owners are linked to a blacklisted private equity firm.

Export control is now enforced by headline as well as by licence, and that raises the political risk attached to every allocation of Nvidia capacity. AUNZ buyers, including sovereign AI programmes and research clouds, compete for the same supply. The second finding is the more useful one for them: an approval on paper is not evidence of a counterparty, and due diligence on a broker is now part of procurement.

Anthropic will list at a cut valuation into the backlash the midterms produced

Anthropic is preparing a Nasdaq listing, likely in mid-November, and investors see a valuation of $1 trillion to $1.5 trillion, down from the $2 trillion floated in recent months. Its amended prospectus discloses about $895.4 billion of purchase commitments and leases, substantially all for compute and infrastructure. The Nasdaq had its steepest one-day fall in 19 months the previous Thursday. In the midterms, voters rejected data-centre ballot measures in 40 of 41 cases, and an NBC News exit poll found more than half called data centres a crucial or major factor in their vote. Seventy-one per cent of Americans opposed a local AI data centre in a Gallup survey in March.

This is the first time a pure-play AI company has had to price political opposition into an equity story. The $895 billion commitment is the part that matters to AUNZ readers, because it is capacity contracted years ahead against a public mood that can delay the sites. Institutional investors here with private-market exposure should read the risk-factor section, which already names organised opposition to data-centre development.

South-east Asia's datacentre boom hit a power and water wall

Thailand paused construction on 49 datacentre projects and approval decisions on 117 more in September, over insufficient detail for regulation and monitoring. The governor of West Java halted a project for want of permits and an incomplete environmental assessment. The pause follows a year of extraordinary growth: operational capacity in Malaysia's Johor rose from 94 megawatts in 2023 to 1,110 megawatts in 2026, with 3,088 megawatts more planned. Wood Mackenzie estimates Johor's datacentres already use 24 per cent of the state's power, rising to 40 per cent by 2035. Australian operator AirTrunk plans to spend $3 billion doubling its Johor sites to four.

This is the sequence Australia is entering, compressed into two years. Capacity arrives before the grid connection and the water allocation, and the objections follow the construction. The NSW upper-house inquiry reported on 5 November that its planning framework assesses each project alone, while the cumulative effects land on one substation. AUNZ operators should read a signed power purchase agreement as the real approval, and a planning consent as the weaker one.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
