Executive Summary 6 stories

Top stories. The window's three stories are all about the moment an AI system's output stops being a vendor's claim and becomes somebody else's evidence. First, copyright's case against the frontier labs put a household name on the docket: on Friday 23 October the licensing group that controls Elvis Presley's catalogue sued Anthropic, alleging it trained Claude on 274 Presley songs and seeking up to US$150,000 a track — about US$41 million — in a filing first reported on 24 October. Second, the neutrality claim met its first large-scale measurement: a study by Convovo, a conversational-AI research studio led by Cornell's Tapan Parikh and reported on 24 October, found that when forced to name a candidate across almost 500 US midterm races, xAI's Grok 4.7 chose the Republican in 92 per cent of answers and OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol the Democrat in 97 per cent, with the two models agreeing in fewer than 10 per cent of races. Third, an election went to the polls with AI as a campaign tool rather than a campaign issue: days before Israel's 27 October general election, both blocs were pushing AI-generated video on X, and Haaretz reported that dozens of bots built to target the Iranian government had been repurposed to spread pro-Netanyahu propaganda.

Three things follow. First, the copyright fight has moved from the labels to the estate, and the allegation that matters is not the scraping but the guardrails — the complaint argues Anthropic added them "after being caught" and knew they did not work, which is the difference between a licensing dispute and a claim of wilful infringement. Second, "the model refuses to take a side" is now a testable claim and it failed in both directions at once — not one model leaning but two leaning hard and opposite, which is the hardest version of the finding to explain away as noise, and the ~1,240 tokens riding unseen on every Grok API call is the sort of detail that becomes a regulator's exhibit. Third, the AI election has arrived in a democracy mid-campaign using cheap tools — repurposed influence bots and generated video, no frontier capability required — which is the risk picture AUNZ electoral authorities should be reading rather than the frontier-model one.

Geopolitical context & the arc. Three centres of gravity. Washington was busiest, and in opposite directions: it hardened the bloc line, telling partners they "can't be trusted" in Pax Silica while engaging a rival AI vision, as the US-led group and China's World AI Cooperation Organization filled out their memberships; and, at home, it turned on the industry's own messengers, with lawmakers from both parties calling AI chief executives a political liability. London was quiet on instruments but loud on consequences, with the UK's data-centre build-out the subject of a national examination of what happens to the people who live beside it. Canberra and Wellington were the quiet end of the ledger again — no new instrument, no ministerial statement, an AUNZ duty layer checked and silent, and New Zealand inside an election with advance voting opening on Monday 26 October. The arc: expect the copyright and the political-liability threads to converge — the same labs being sued over training data are being told by the politicians who will write their rules to stop talking, and the AUNZ copyright consultation now has a live American precedent to price. And expect the bloc question to reach AUNZ procurement, because Pax Silica against WAICO is ultimately a question about which model stack a government can buy from.

Elvis Presley's rights-holders sued Anthropic over 274 songs, in the first big AI copyright case brought by an artist's estate

Authentic Brands Group, the licensing group that controls most of Elvis Presley's catalogue together with his likeness and trademarks, and co-plaintiffs sued Anthropic, alleging it trained its Claude models on 274 Presley songs without permission. The complaint — first reported by Billboard on 24 October and carried by The Guardian on 25 October — names "Can't Help Falling in Love", "All Shook Up", "A Little Less Conversation", "Don't Be Cruel" and "Return to Sender" among the works, and seeks statutory damages of up to US$150,000 per infringed work, about US$41 million. Lead attorney Richard Busch writes that Anthropic "has built its entire business model based on copyright infringement at a scale never seen before", and the filing argues that the company's guardrails "to purportedly limit outputs which infringe upon music copyrights, after being caught", with its knowledge that they were ineffective, demonstrate wilful infringement. The Guardian describes it as one of the first major challenges brought directly by an artist's estate rather than by labels or publishers, though Anthropic already faces suits from Universal, Sony and Warner; Anthropic had not commented when the trade press went to print. Why it matters: the training-data question Australia is consulting on — the Attorney-General's copyright consultation, and the joint AI committee's October advice that "current copyright arrangements are not facilitating licensing deals for Australian culture and content" — now has a live American precedent, and the part of it that will travel is the willfulness argument, because it turns on what a developer knew about its own outputs rather than on whether the training happened. The facts here are a filed complaint and the reporting around it; the allegations are untested and Anthropic has not responded.

Forced to choose, Grok leaned Republican in 92 per cent of US races and GPT leaned Democratic in 97 per cent, a study found

A study by Convovo, a conversational-AI research studio led by Cornell University professor Tapan Parikh, asked xAI's Grok 4.7 and OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol through their developer APIs to name a single candidate in this year's US midterm races — "Name one candidate. Don't hedge, waffle, or list both sides without deciding." Across nearly 500 races, Grok recommended the Republican in 92 per cent of answers and GPT the Democrat in 97 per cent, and the two models agreed in fewer than 10 per cent of races, TechCrunch reported on 24 October. The consumer apps refused to answer, which an OpenAI spokesperson said was significant because that is how most people meet the models; OpenAI also said ChatGPT "is designed to be objective by default, including in political conversations" and that a developer API with an explicitly forcing prompt does not reflect the product. xAI did not respond. The study found the models citing different sources — Grok surfaced a Christian conservative voter guide in 39 per cent of answers, Turning Point Action in 23 per cent and X in 41 per cent, while GPT leaned on Wikipedia, the Associated Press, Axios and Reddit — and searching for different issues, GPT on healthcare and abortion, Grok on immigration, taxes, crime and energy. Every request to Grok through xAI's API carried about 1,240 tokens of additional input the researchers did not write and could not see or remove, which Parikh called potentially "hidden instructions". Pew found on 23 October that only 8 per cent of Americans use chatbots for election information. Why it matters: it converts a general worry about model bias into a measured divergence, and the direction is the finding — two frontier models disagreeing in nine races out of ten means at least one is systematically wrong for most of the country it is being asked about. For AUNZ readers the read is about the API layer rather than the apps: the bias appears where organisations embed these models into their own products, and the unexplained per-request tokens are the kind of artefact an electoral commission would ask about. The study is a single, not-yet-peer-reviewed piece of research by one studio, and its numbers are the researchers' own.

Israel's election campaign ran on AI deepfakes and repurposed influence bots

Days before Israel's 27 October general election, both main blocs were using AI-generated video on X as campaign material, and a repurposed influence network was amplifying one of them. Al Jazeera reported on 25 October that the camps of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot — locked in a virtual tie in the polls — were pushing AI-made videos at each other, including fabricated footage of Netanyahu confronting his alleged failings before the 7 October 2023 attack, and material casting the opposition as sympathetic to the Arab parties that could decide the next coalition. On the Thursday, the Israeli daily Haaretz reported that dozens of online bots built to undermine the Iranian government had been repurposed to spread campaign propaganda for Netanyahu's Likud and warnings about the opposition. An earlier AI video shared by Netanyahu showed Eisenkot passing a figure resembling his son, who was killed in Gaza; the campaign said the resemblance was unintended and replaced the character. Roi Zur, a voting-behaviour specialist at Essex University, told Al Jazeera that AI, algorithms and data management are being used to cement "affective polarisation". Why it matters: it is the deepfake election arriving in a democracy without a deepfake-disclosure duty — unlike the European Union, where Article 50(4) of the AI Act has required labelling since August 2026, Israel is voting without a comparable obligation, so the videos ran undisclosed. That is the AUNZ read: the harm requires no frontier capability, only a generative model and an audience, which is why electoral authorities and platforms cannot treat it as a future problem. The report is a major international broadcaster's, grounded in a national daily's investigation and named experts; the videos are public artefacts, and no regulator has opened a proceeding.

4

The data centre next door: the people living beside Britain's AI build-out say consent was never asked for

The Guardian reported on 24 October from Newport in south Wales, where a 23-metre-high hyperscale data centre stands beside an enclave of 27 houses. Residents describe losing light and quiet to 36 air-blast coolers and to regular testing of 60 back-up diesel generators, and the council's own chief scientific officer warned during planning that the generators' fumes had the "potential to cause unacceptable air pollution levels". The piece sets the local case inside a national one: 315 hyperscale data centres are queued for connection to the UK grid, requiring 73GW of power — 28GW above Britain's typical cold-winter peak demand of 45GW — while first the Starmer and then the Burnham governments kept the five AI growth zones and rejected calls for a moratorium. Globally, the Guardian cites estimates of up to US$7 trillion in data-centre spending by 2030, more than the New Deal, the Marshall Plan and the Apollo programme combined in real terms. In the United States, it notes, three-quarters of Americans now oppose local data-centre development and more than 500 counties and municipalities have passed bans or moratoria. Why it matters: it is the social-licence bill for the AI build-out arriving as ordinary planning politics, which is the same ground Australia's proposed mandatory standards for large data centres occupy — bring-your-own generation, a full share of connection costs, and demand reduction when the grid is tight — and the objection it answers is less economic than about who was asked. The report is the Guardian's own, with named residents and cited public documents; the pollution warning is a planning-process finding, not a regulatory one.

Washington told partners they cannot sit in both AI camps, as the US and China filled out rival blocs

The Associated Press reported on 25 October that the two AI powers are building competing blocs and narrowing the room for everyone else. The US-led Pax Silica, which pairs supply-chain security with access to American chips and models, is set against China's World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO), whose founding membership includes Pakistan and Russia. The US State Department hardened the line: "Countries can't be trusted partners in Pax Silica while substantively engaging in initiatives that advance a competing vision for super intelligence." Kazakhstan has joined both — its AI minister, Zhaslan Madiyev, went to Washington in June and to Shanghai for a WAICO signing ceremony weeks later — while Indonesia has joined the Chinese grouping and wants to stay in both, and Singapore says it is considering WAICO. The AP cites AidData research that China has provided grants and loans for AI-related projects in at least 77 low- and middle-income countries, and analysts who argue that "swing countries" now hold unusual leverage. Why it matters: it is the strategic layer of the question every AUNZ buyer answers operationally — which model stack, which chips and which cloud a government can depend on — and it is being settled by bloc membership rather than by procurement. For AUNZ readers the signal is that the open-weight, price-competitive Chinese stack is being marketed to middle powers as the alternative to Pax Silica's conditions, which is a real fork for small jurisdictions with US treaty ties. The reporting is the AP's, sourced to named analysts at Brookings, CSIS and Harvard and to on-record government statements; the bloc framing is those sources' analysis.

Washington's message to AI executives: you have become a political liability

Axios reported on 24 October that lawmakers and White House advisers from both parties now regard AI chief executives as a "monumental political liability", because they oscillate between promises of prosperity and warnings of doom. Representative Ro Khanna told Axios that "CEOs like Sam Altman are saying the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology — you cannot trust billionaires to make decisions to keep us safe", while Senator John Curtis said AI leaders are "tripping over their own shoelaces". The outlet reports that even officials sympathetic to the industry want executives to focus on shipping products rather than making sweeping existential pronouncements, and frames the founders' catastrophic rhetoric as making the regulatory conversation harder rather than easier. Why it matters: the people who will write the next round of AI rules are now saying publicly that the industry's own messaging is the obstacle, which is a change in the political weather worth more than any single bill — and it lands in a fortnight when the US Federal Trade Commission had already opened an inquiry into autonomous agents and a state attorney-general had sued a platform over AI-assisted fraud. For AUNZ readers it is a reminder that the regulatory risk to AI firms is increasingly political rather than legal, which shapes how much they will concede before legislation arrives. The report rests on on-record comments from named lawmakers; the "political liability" characterisation is Axios's own.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
