Research & evidence 1 story

1

METR finds Anthropic's models improving at about twice the historical rate

METR published an independent assessment on 5 November of how fast AI is speeding up AI research at Anthropic. Its staff worked inside the company from 12 August to 29 October. METR concluded that Anthropic is likely improving its models on well-specified tasks at about twice the rate it managed between March 2024 and October 2025. It put the plausible range at 1.3 to 3 times. Benchmark scores have grown at 1.9 times the pre-October-2025 rate, which implies roughly two years of progress in one. METR judged that about 80 per cent of the current acceleration comes from AI. It ruled out faster growth in training compute as the cause. Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR found most informative.

This is the first independent measurement of the loop frontier labs describe in their own risk reports. Anthropic's rules treat a doubling of progress as an early warning threshold. METR says that threshold may already have been crossed, and that the public report understates what was happening. The redactions matter for Australian readers as much as for researchers. A system card nobody can audit is a governance claim, not a control.

AI security & agentic risk 2 stories

2

OpenAI graded its new cyber model Critical and gated the access

OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber on 5 November, a model trained to find and validate exploitable vulnerabilities. It scores 48.5 per cent on ExploitGym, up from 32.1 per cent for GPT-5.6 Cyber. On a new remote-exploitation benchmark it completed 95 per cent of tasks that its predecessor refused outright. OpenAI graded the model Critical for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework, up from High. The company reports two results already. Its researchers used the model to build 948 privilege-escalation exploits in Linux. It also found 81 previously unknown high-severity vulnerabilities in Firefox, and Mozilla published fixes for many of them on 27 October. Access runs through the Daybreak programme, which requires vetting.

The governance question is not whether the model is capable. It is who holds it. OpenAI has graded a model Critical for offensive cyber work, then sold access to vetted defenders rather than publishing the weights. That concentrates capability on one side of the fight and makes a vendor's vetting a security control. The scope training is the other half. The model stayed inside its authorised boundaries in 99.93 per cent of tests without a deployment prompt, against 94.49 per cent for its predecessor.

3

Australia's cyber agency published guidance on running open-weight models

The Australian Signals Directorate's cyber centre published two guides on open-weight AI models on 5 November. The first sets out what open-weight models are, what they offer, and the security and supply-chain risks that come with releasing model weights publicly. The second covers governance, model selection, secure deployment, monitoring, maintenance and incident response for organisations running them. The ASD frames the trade plainly. Open weights give an organisation more control over its data and operations. They also load that organisation with responsibility for provenance, patching, monitoring and response.

Most Australian enterprises now run at least one open-weight model, and the guidance describes what they have already taken on. A closed API pushes patching and abuse monitoring to the vendor. A downloaded checkpoint does not. Read the provenance section first, because a fine-tuned public model carries no vendor warranty and no upstream alerting. Nothing here is enforceable, so the practical value is that an agency asking what good looks like now has a named ASD answer to point at.

Market & geopolitics 1 story

4

Tech stocks had their worst day since April 2025 as investors priced a datacentre slowdown

US technology stocks fell sharply on 5 November, a day after rallying on the midterm result. The Nasdaq Composite closed 5.52 per cent lower at 24,315, more than 10 per cent below its record close. The S&P 500 lost 4.36 per cent and the Dow fell 1,833 points. All three recorded their worst session since April 2025. A semiconductor index dropped more than 12 per cent, its worst day since March 2020. Nvidia fell 9 per cent and Broadcom 10 per cent. The neocloud operators fell further, with Nebius about 15 per cent and Iren almost 17. Investors pointed to Democratic wins and a Senate call for a national datacentre moratorium.

The selloff is the market discovering that a ballot measure is a capital-expenditure risk. The day before, it had read the same result as a spending tailwind. Australia has no equivalent of a county referendum, so the direct mechanism differs. The funding mechanism does not. A hyperscaler that must now justify a campus to a governor will price that risk into every jurisdiction, including ones that have not voted. Watch the debt markets rather than the index, because that is where campus financing actually clears.

Regulation & obligation 1 story

5

Victoria's campaign is being fought with AI-generated video of both leaders

Both major parties in Victoria are using AI-generated video of their opponents in the state campaign. Victorian Labor posted a clip on 31 October showing opposition leader Jess Wilson trick-or-treating with invented warnings about American-style healthcare. The Liberals have posted AI video of Premier Ben Carroll asleep at his desk. Advance Australia, a conservative lobby group, released an AI-made election-night victory that never happened. Only that video is labelled as generated. Victoria has no truth-in-political-advertising law. South Australia bans unlabelled AI electoral ads that show a person doing something they did not do, with fines of up to $5,000. The Greens want the same rules in Victoria.

Australia is writing national AI rules while its state campaigns test what is already possible without them. The South Australian model is the only local precedent, and it is narrow. It covers fabricated conduct, not misleading emphasis, so a video that puts real words in a real person's mouth sits closer to the edge than the text of the ban implies. For anyone advising a party, a union or a lobby group this cycle, the exposure is reputational, and in two jurisdictions financial.

Infrastructure & compute 1 story

6

OpenAI published campus commitments and asked lawmakers to turn them into rules

OpenAI published a National Community Compact and a compute-campus policy blueprint on 5 November. The compact sets out what communities can expect at campuses the company owns and operates. It promises to pay the full cost of the power and infrastructure it uses, with financial assurances before major spending begins. It also commits to a water plan for each site, published water and electricity use, and no NDAs with public officials. The blueprint asks state utility commissions to make large customers cover the grid costs they add. It also proposes public reporting on water, energy, noise and hiring, and recovery of tax incentives when a developer falls short.

This is a vendor drafting the regulation it would rather face. The commitments are voluntary and reach only sites OpenAI owns and operates, which excludes much of the capacity it has contracted. The NDA clause is the one with teeth, because secrecy conditions are how a campus deal stays invisible until the bulldozers arrive. The blueprint's grid-cost ask would bind OpenAI's competitors too. For AUNZ host communities and state planners it is a usable template, offered before anyone legislated it.

Frontier models & capability claims 1 story

7

Google cut the price of its video model by 30 per cent and lengthened its clips

Google made Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash generally available on 5 November. The model now generates fifteen-second clips natively and holds a reference image more consistently across a shot. It can be edited through a multi-turn conversation rather than a fresh prompt each time. Google cut the price 30 per cent against version 1.1, to seven US cents per second of 720p video. That puts a thirty-second clip at about US$2.10. The model is available through the Gemini API and Google Cloud. The company reports better reference adherence and publishes no independent evaluation of it.

Video generation has been priced as a novelty and is now being priced as a utility. A thirty-second clip for two dollars is inside the budget of a small marketing team and far below a shoot. The capability that matters commercially is not clip length but reference consistency, because brand work fails when the product changes shape between shots. AUNZ production and advertising firms should read the cost curve, not the demo reel.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Rendered only when the band is not None; a pill reading “Obligation: None” on every card is noise. Proposed means text exists; Signalled means a minister or a government has said it will — a two-stage ministerial statement stays Signalled. Where an obligation is claimed, the card names the instrument and its operative provision.

Evidence — how established is the claim?

Orthogonal to verification, and the reason this product exists separately from the cyber digest: an AI capability claim usually cannot be verified at all. A vendor testifying about its own model is Verified (they did say it) and simultaneously a Vendor claim (the capability is unreplicated). Both badges render.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
