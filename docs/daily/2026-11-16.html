1

Italy fined Meta €805 million and gave it sixty days to ask for consent properly

The Garante, Italy's data protection authority, fined Meta Platforms Ireland €805 million on 14 November. It is the largest fine the authority has imposed. The decision concerns a Facebook for Android feature called "Get suggestions from your gallery while browsing Facebook", available since 2023. The app asked for one permission covering the whole app, behind a generic notice about "enabling other functions". Photos, videos and their metadata were read from about 20 million Android phones in Italy without specific consent. The amount is about 0.47 per cent of Meta's global revenue. Meta must switch the feature off by default within 60 days and seek consent separately, and report its initiatives within 30 days and the measures adopted within 90. The investigation opened in July 2025 after a complaint from a consumer group in Bolzano. The Garante applied article 122 of Italy's privacy code, which transposes the ePrivacy directive, and rejected Meta's argument that Ireland's regulator should handle the case. Meta says it will appeal.

The defect is the single permission, not the feature. One app-wide grant covering several functions is what many mobile applications still ask for, and the Garante has now put a price on it. For Australian and New Zealand teams the parallel is domestic. The Privacy Act requires notice of what is collected and why, at the point of collection, and an AI feature that is on by default and explained in a general settings screen fails that test on this reasoning.

The US Treasury told Nvidia's board to hire an outsider and answer within ten days

Scott Bessent, the US Treasury Secretary, said on Fox News on 15 November that Nvidia's board should look outside the company for its next chief executive. "This makes me think that as the board conducts a search for a new leader, that they need to go outside the company," he said. He demanded answers within ten days from the board and its outside counsel. The questions concern whether Nvidia knowingly pursued what he called "seemingly illicit" chip sales to China through shell companies, many of them in Singapore. He compared the case to a bank that had failed its compliance obligations and would have had executives removed. Bessent separately rejected calls to write tighter export controls into law, saying "we have all the rules we need". Congress is working on a bipartisan export bill that could pass within weeks. Nvidia has hired Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to lead its own investigation and reports quarterly results on Tuesday.

The argument has moved from export rules to corporate governance. A cabinet secretary is asking whether a compliance function failed and at what level, which is the test banks face after a sanctions breach. That framing travels: an Australian or New Zealand buyer of GPU capacity is now assessing a supplier with an interim chief executive and an open compliance question. The ten days is a political deadline rather than a legal one, and the board can miss it without consequence.

Asia-Pacific governments re-checked their Nvidia deals after the chief executive resigned

TechCrunch surveyed the region's response on 14 November. Singapore's home ministry said it had barred Alice Huang from re-entering the country. Police found insufficient evidence in February 2026 that she committed offences over Megaspeed's server declarations, but concluded she had knowingly given inaccurate information about her role. Her passport was returned and a warning issued, and the investigation into Megaspeed and six other people continues. South Korea's science ministry said Nvidia had reaffirmed a plan to supply 260,000 advanced GPUs and to continue joint AI projects, and the government's AI strategy council met on Friday. Taiwan's economy minister said the leadership change was unlikely to alter the industry's growth, and Taipei said Nvidia's planned headquarters there remains on track. Japan's industry ministry said it would monitor the company closely. China's foreign ministry rejected the espionage allegations as groundless and said it was not aware of reports tying restricted chips to a Shanghai data centre.

Sovereign buyers responded by asking a supplier to re-confirm its contracts, and that is the template. Australia has no comparable GPU commitment to re-confirm, which is itself the exposure: the domestic buildout rests on commercial contracts that no government has underwritten. The Singapore finding also matters here, because it is the first regulator to put a conclusion on the record about the company at the centre of the case.

Infrastructure & compute 1 story

4

A US judge let a memory-price conspiracy case proceed, with the AI buildout as its subject

Judge Noël Wise of the Northern District of California refused on 14 November to dismiss a class action against Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron. The suit was filed on 25 June 2026 by 17 consumers and small businesses. It alleges the three companies, which held more than 91 per cent of global DRAM revenue in the third quarter of 2025, cut conventional memory production from late 2022. They then moved wafer capacity to high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators, which needs three to four times the wafer area per gigabyte. Conventional DRAM prices rose 171.8 per cent in a year by that quarter, and the complaint alleges 697 per cent over 18 months. None of the three expanded capacity. The companies argued the case repeated a 2016 to 2018 action that the Ninth Circuit dismissed. Judge Wise disagreed, finding the allegations "more akin to hawks flying in formation". Answers are due on 7 December.

The constraint on AI compute is now a legal question as well as a commercial one. If the case reaches discovery, the high-bandwidth memory contracts behind every accelerator order become evidence. For Australian and New Zealand buyers the effect is timing, because a ruling that returned capacity to commodity memory would move prices already built into data-centre business cases. The allegations are untested, and a denied motion to dismiss decides nothing about the merits.

5

Stanford published code for a memory system that hands a frozen model sixteen tokens

The SNAP group at Stanford released Memorilla on 14 November, with code and a training recipe under Apache 2.0. The system reads a document collection once and stores it as reusable embeddings. For each question it learns to place what matters into a fixed set of 16 memory tokens in the model's input, rather than pasting documents into the context window. It runs against a frozen Qwen3-8B, so the base model is never retrained. The paper, posted to arXiv on 15 October and revised on 3 November, reports the best score on five of eight benchmarks against Mem0 and MemoRAG. On FactKG it reports 84.33 per cent accuracy against 80.43 per cent for full-context inference, using 108 times fewer tokens. In a TextWorld agent test it lifts success from 18.6 to 24.2 per cent.

The claim worth testing is that 16 tokens can beat the full document set. That cuts against the assumption that longer context is the answer to memory, and it points at where agents lose track over long runs. The numbers are the authors' own, on their own benchmarks, and nobody else has replicated them. For an organisation weighing retrieval against long context, the useful step is the same either way: run the comparison on your own corpus before moving a production system.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
