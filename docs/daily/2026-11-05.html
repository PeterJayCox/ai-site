Market & geopolitics 2 stories

1

American voters rejected data centres in 40 of 41 local ballots

Voters in 10 US states decided 41 local ballot measures on data centres on 3 November. They backed restrictions, or rejected pro-development measures, in every contest but one, according to a tally by Ballotpedia and CNBC. The exception was Harwood, North Dakota, where a charter amendment requiring extra review before industrial approvals was voted down. Ohio carried 21 of the measures. Democrat Sherrod Brown unseated Republican senator Jon Husted after casting him as "the face of data centres". Democrat Amy Acton won the governor's race promising a conditional moratorium, under which operators would cover all utility costs. The state hosts the PORTS-Pike campus, an 8-gigawatt project developed by SoftBank and SB Energy with the Department of Energy, and backstopped by Nvidia. It could cost more than US$500 billion.

A county ballot can now stop a project that a utility contract has already financed, and that is a new kind of risk for anyone underwriting AI capacity. Australia is writing the same bargain into national standards for large data centres, with power, water and grid conditions attached. The Ohio result is the argument for doing it early. Once a community has voted, the negotiation is no longer about conditions.

2

Canva cut 1,271 roles, a fifth of the company, after its first growth slowdown in a decade

Canva told staff on 3 November that 1,271 roles, about 22 per cent of the company, would be cut, changed or moved. Melanie Perkins, the chief executive, said some people would lose their jobs and others would be asked to relocate to centralised hubs. The note merges the product, growth and platform teams into one product organisation and removes layers of sub-specialisation. Everyone leaving receives at least six months of salary, keeps their company laptop, and has the one-year vesting cliff removed. Perkins called it the hardest moment in the company's thirteen years. She said Canva's growth had slowed for the first time in a decade, and that new technology was changing what customers create and expect. The Australian Associated Press reported that Canva ruled out replacing staff with AI.

Australia's best-known technology company has now run the restructuring its peers ran earlier, and it arrived as a reorganisation rather than a substitution. That distinction will not survive contact with the market. For local employers the sequence is the useful part: headcount moves first, and any measured productivity gain comes later, if it comes at all.

Regulation & obligation 1 story

3

An integrity think tank told the government it is sleepwalking into a second robodebt

The Centre for Public Integrity published a report on 5 November calling for a statutory framework covering automated decision-making across government. Its eight recommendations include a mandatory audit of systems already in use, a public transparency register, and an independent oversight body able to investigate agencies. It also wants parliament to authorise categories of automated statutory decisions, and legal scrutiny of each system before deployment. Catherine Williams, the centre's chief executive, said Australia was "sleepwalking into another robodebt scenario". The report follows an October finding by the Commonwealth Ombudsman that the aged care assessment tool under-assessed people's needs, and that assessors were barred from overriding it. The Attorney-General's Department has been drafting an ADM framework since consultation closed in January 2025. No draft has been released.

The report lands five weeks before a transparency obligation begins under the Privacy Act. From 10 December, APP 1 requires an organisation to say in its privacy policy how automated decisions are made. That covers disclosure, not lawfulness, human review or appeal, which is the gap the centre is describing. For any AUNZ agency or insurer running a scoring or triage model, the immediate deadline is the privacy policy. The harder question is who can explain a decision after it has been made.

Frontier models & capability claims 1 story

4

OpenAI says GPT-6 Astra designed a working device end to end, in under a week

OpenAI said on 3 November that two of its staff built a working device with GPT-6 Astra in under a week, as a side project. The model wrote the functional requirements and designed the enclosure, the circuit board, the interface and the firmware that connects the device to Codex. A contract manufacturer built a small batch of prototypes in under a month. OpenAI also reported the limits. Astra did not consistently account for the manufacturer's production limits, tolerances or assembly details, and getting the device ready for manufacture took several rounds of review.

This is a vendor describing its own model, so the week-long build is a demonstration rather than a measurement. The part worth keeping is the failure mode. Design is now a task a model can attempt end to end, and manufacturing is where it still needs a person in the loop. That is a useful map for AUNZ engineering firms deciding where to put the model and where to keep the sign-off.

AI security & agentic risk 1 story

5

Apple told investors that AI agents may act in unintended or unpredictable ways

Apple added AI agents to the risk factors in its annual report, filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission on 3 November. The company wrote that agents can act autonomously, including by reaching systems they may not be authorised to access, executing or altering code, sending communications and completing transactions. It said they "may behave in unintended or unpredictable ways, including by taking actions that were not directed, instructed or authorized". Apple warned the behaviour could expose it to litigation and reputational harm. The disclosure follows a year of agent incidents, including an agent that left its sandbox at Hugging Face. Apple changed full disk access on Macs last month to reduce the risk from locally installed agents.

A risk factor is a legal statement rather than an engineering finding, and Apple is protecting itself against lawsuits. It is still the clearest sign that agent risk has moved from research blogs into board disclosures. The control Apple chose is instructive, because it narrowed the permission an agent holds on the machine. The disclosure also names the four things to log and limit in any deployment: system access, code execution, outbound messages and payments.

Infrastructure & compute 1 story

6

Google put another US$15 billion into Malaysia, with conditions attached

Google said on 4 November that it will invest a further US$15 billion in Malaysia across 2027 and 2028. The money expands the Elmina Business Park campus in Selangor and adds campuses in Selangor and Negeri Sembilan. Google projects more than 37,000 local jobs a year during construction, more than 9,000 long-term roles, and an average annual contribution of US$1.1 billion to gross domestic product. The package carries conditions. Google will work with the utility Tenaga Nasional to add more than a gigawatt of clean energy without public subsidy. It will also build an off-river storage reservoir and water treatment plant supplying 65 million litres a day, to be handed to the state. The company will also train 800,000 Malaysians by 2031. Anwar Ibrahim, who attended the announcement, said the conditions had been imposed and accepted.

Malaysia has become the template for trading land, power and water access for local capability, and it is doing so in public with a head of government present. Australia is writing the same bargain into national standards for large data centres. The difference in Malaysia is the breadth of the local commitment, which covers suppliers, training and a water asset that reverts to the state. That is the version of social licence AUNZ host communities are now asking for, and it is being priced into a hyperscaler's own announcement.

Research & evidence 1 story

7

A preprint says the trick that makes models fast also moves their answers

Eight researchers posted a preprint on 3 November arguing that speculative decoding changes what a model answers, not only how fast it answers. Speculative decoding has been treated as a pure speed optimisation, on the assumption that a verified token is a verified token. The authors measured frontier open-weight models on agentic tasks and found numerical noise accumulating inside verification windows. After about 30 steps, they report, the draft model's acceptance decisions shift the output distribution and move accuracy in random directions. They then use that effect. The draft model is trained to be rejected at critical decision points, so verification restarts and the important tokens are sampled from the target model in its least noisy state. On agentic coding benchmarks this raised end-to-end success while training between 0.06 and 0.28 per cent of hosted parameters.

This is one preprint with no replication, and the evaluation is the authors' own. The claim still matters to anyone buying inference. If a serving configuration changes long-horizon accuracy, then a benchmark run on one vendor's stack does not describe the model you deploy on another. It also gives AUNZ buyers a question worth asking of any capability number: which decoding settings produced it.

Key to this page how to read it

Impact — the only coloured axis

How far the risk, obligation or capability position moves. Impact is not harm: half of what this digest carries — a model release, a research result, a funding round — is not a harm at all. The band is driven by precedent, blast radius, reversibility, structural change and sector criticality, not by a severity dictionary borrowed from cyber incidents.

Obligation — does it bind an AUNZ organisation?

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
