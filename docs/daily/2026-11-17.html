Washington put the licence on the computing rather than the shipment

The Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule on 16 November requiring a licence to export, re-export or transfer advanced AI chips to a data centre, wherever it is. The test is whether the exporter knows the facility will supply more than 12 million aggregate TPP of computing power to a company headquartered in China, Macau or another Country Group D:5 destination. A separate provision adds a destination licence for advanced chips sent to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which BIS described as presenting a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. US-headquartered buyers can avoid that destination licence by meeting new reporting conditions. The rule takes effect in 90 days. BIS said it was written at the direction of the Treasury and the National Security Council, and that it closes the route by which existing restrictions could be evaded through remote access.

The previous controls stopped at the border, and a workload rented in a third country was outside them. This rule follows the computing instead. The exposure for Australian and New Zealand buyers is practical rather than legal: capacity sourced through a Singapore or Malaysian data centre now sits behind a licence, and the test turns on what the exporter knew about the ultimate customer. Anyone modelling GPU access into a business case should treat the supply route, not just the supplier, as a variable.

Google watched a patch turn into a working exploit with no human in the loop

Google Threat Intelligence Group reported on 16 November that it had observed threat actors automate vulnerability discovery and exploitation end to end for the first time. The framework, which GTIG calls exploit_agent, sat on an exposed staging server and was wired to several frontier and locally hosted models. It compared web application code before and after each patch announcement, drafted an exploit for a plausible flaw and executed it without intervention. The server also held exfiltrated victim data and scripts for brute-forcing, installing webshells and removing rival attackers' webshells. Victim telemetry pointed at French media, entertainment and education sites, and a configuration comment was written in Russian. GTIG also observed the open-source frameworks CAIRN and PentAGI in live intrusions, and a suspected China-nexus group building its own automated attack framework.

Patch-then-exploit used to be a race a defender could win by patching quickly. The report says attackers can now work from the finite material a patch publishes, and that hunting n-day bugs is becoming cheaper than hunting zero-days. GTIG's own caveat is that these frameworks trade stealth for speed, because unconstrained execution leaves forensic traces. That is the control that still works: detect the noise, and shorten the interval between a patch and its deployment rather than assuming a monthly cycle.

3

Anthropic cut its bug-disclosure window to 30 days because models find flaws faster

Anthropic will shorten its coordinated vulnerability disclosure window from 90 days to 30 for high and critical severity issues. Everything else moves to 45 days. Both apply to reports made on or after 1 January 2027. The company will escalate to an external coordinator after 15 days rather than 30 when a maintainer does not answer. It will publish full technical detail 30 days after a patch, down from 45. Anthropic said in a post on 16 November that models have become much better at finding vulnerabilities, and that a bug one model finds is now likely to be found by another. High and critical reports already past the old deadline have gone unpublished; those maintainers are being given 30 more days before the reports appear on Anthropic's dashboard.

The 90-day norm was written when a fix moved at human speed and a second party was unlikely to find the same bug. Neither holds now, and the practical effect of 30 days is that a maintainer's repair window lines up with a monthly patch cadence rather than a quarterly one. This is a vendor setting the clock for third-party software, not a regulator, which is why it matters for anyone shipping open-source dependencies: the publication date of a proof of concept has moved closer to the patch.

OpenAI gave four outside labs the keys to its training runs

OpenAI said on 16 November it had signed agreements with Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire for a first phase of embedded assessments. Under the arrangements, assessors work on site and receive access to model checkpoints, source code, training and evaluation data, monitoring systems and the researchers responsible for the work. Apollo will test four claims about whether models could covertly work against their developers and whether safeguards would detect it. METR will examine misaligned behaviour in OpenAI's largest reinforcement learning run to date. Transluce will assess the quality and coverage of post-training alignment evaluations. Goodfire will embed interpretability researchers and publish findings as the work proceeds. OpenAI's Safety and Security Committee receives unredacted reports and resolves disagreements between the company and an assessor.

The interesting part is the dispute mechanism rather than the access list. An assessor who can escalate to a board committee, and can publish that OpenAI declined to attest to a statement, has a route to report a failure the company disputes. The commitments also carry an obligation: OpenAI ties them to the White House Accord on Super Intelligence, where independent assessment of safety controls was part of the bargain. Australian and New Zealand regulators have no equivalent lever, which is why the 10 November announcement here promises evaluation access by statute.

5

Models dropped settled climate facts the moment a user pushed back

UNESCO and University College London published a study on 16 November, launched at the COP31 summit in Antalya, testing 15 of the most popular AI models on climate questions. The models confirmed simple facts reliably. When a user mildly disagreed, they abandoned the correct answer in about eight wrongful pivots out of ten and affirmed false statements such as "CO2 is not the main driver of climate change". Asked how two climate policies would interact, accuracy fell to no better than random guessing. Accuracy was 88 per cent for English-language users and 78 per cent for Swahili speakers, the lowest of the languages tested. The report's authors describe the behaviour as sycophancy, and describe the systems as weak and fallible.

The failure is deference, not ignorance, which is the harder defect to test for. A model that will restate the correct answer will also restate whatever the person in front of it prefers, and that applies to any advisory setting where the ground truth is settled and the user is wrong. For agencies and banks putting a model in front of the public, the control is a test that includes adversarial disagreement, not one that asks factual questions only.

Hugging Face handed its inference routing to OpenRouter

Hugging Face switched its Inference Providers service from its own router to OpenRouter's on 16 November. Requests to language models on the Hub are now routed across more than 80 providers on live price, speed and reliability measurements, with automatic retry to another provider when one fails. Existing code is unchanged and a Hugging Face token works across every model those providers serve. The router supports OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses APIs and Anthropic's Messages API for chat models, regardless of which provider is behind them.

Open weights had a distribution problem rather than a model problem, and this merges two of the layers that solve it. For a team running open models, the practical change is that provider selection becomes a runtime decision instead of a procurement one, and one token covers the catalogue. The cost is concentration: two intermediaries now sit between an open model and its user, and neither party published traffic or price data with the announcement.

The campaign to reinstate Jensen Huang picked up the president

Donald Trump told reporters on 16 November that he would "certainly not mind seeing" Jensen Huang return as Nvidia's chief executive, and that Huang had "learned a big lesson". He spoke as an open letter signed by Elon Musk and other technology executives passed 165,000 signatures, asking the board to bring Huang back while an independent investigation establishes the facts. Huang resigned on 12 November after reports of extramarital relationships, one with a woman alleged to be a Chinese intelligence asset, having admitted days earlier to being unfaithful to his wife. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive. The Financial Times reports that Huang has discussed a return with people close to him, and that allies are assembling a lobbying effort.

A supplier with an interim chief executive and a public campaign around its leadership is a procurement risk, and the campaign now has the government that writes its export licences behind it. Trump also said nobody had proved the woman was a spy, which puts the White House on one side of an investigation the board's independent directors opened. For buyers of GPU capacity in Australia and New Zealand, the question is not who runs Nvidia but whether the licence regime in the card above is being written by people who have taken a position on its management.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
