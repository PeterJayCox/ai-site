Australia and Singapore signed a dedicated AI cooperation statement

The Prime Ministers of Australia and Singapore signed a joint statement on AI on 24 November, on the occasion of the 11th Singapore–Australia Annual Leaders' Meeting. It sits alongside a separate leaders' joint statement and the Prime Minister's Canberra press conference transcript the same day. The AI statement reaffirms the 2024 MoU on AI Cooperation and the refreshed 2026 MoU on Cyber Security Cooperation, and welcomes the inaugural Cyber and Digital Senior Officials' Dialogue held in July. It commits the two governments to exchanges on AI testing, evaluation and assurance, and on workforce capability. It also commits to cooperation on international AI governance through the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science, and to supporting ASEAN's 2027 chair-year work on AI testing standards. It adds support for Pacific-led AI capabilities, drawing on Singapore's SEA-LION multilingual model and Project Atlas.

Why it matters

This is a channel, not a rule, and it commits neither government to anything enforceable. Its value is that it names the technical bodies — testing, evaluation and assurance — rather than the usual aspiration language, and it does so ahead of Australia's AI Standards legislation in early 2027. Singapore runs some of the region's most developed AI testing infrastructure, so a testing-and-assurance exchange is a concrete thing to build on. Australian and New Zealand firms that sell AI into the region should watch whether the dialogue produces a published work programme, not just a photograph.

AI security & agentic risk 2 stories

2

Amazon Bedrock now ships Anthropic's enterprise safeguards through one API

Amazon Web Services announced general availability of its trusted access program API on 23 November, with two Anthropic offerings in public preview. Enterprise Frontier Safeguards keeps a customer's prompts and outputs in the customer's own AWS account, encrypted with the customer's own KMS key, and Bedrock reaches that key and bucket as a single service principal through policies the customer writes. If the customer removes that access, Bedrock stops serving the covered models 24 hours later. Automated safety monitoring looks for patterns across many requests, including offensive-cyber use and agents that go beyond what a user asked for, and sends flags to the customer. Neither AWS nor Anthropic reviews the content, and the data is never used for training. Enterprise Data Controls stores retained data in Amazon-managed storage instead, with prompts and completions not retained by Anthropic. Both offerings cover Claude Fable, Mythos, Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 used through the Cyber Verification Program.

Why it matters

The design answers the retention question that OpenRouter's disclosure made concrete on 22 November: when a reseller logs prompts, the customer usually finds out last. Here the customer holds the key and the bucket, the monitoring is automated, and the flags go to the customer's own security team rather than to the vendor. The architecture is the product, and it is auditable — the customer's own IAM policies define who can enroll. Enterprises in regulated industries now have a documented pattern for frontier-model access that does not rest on a vendor's retention promise. Whether the monitoring is any good is a separate question, and Anthropic's own post concedes it is still being tuned.

3

OpenAI and Cisco open-sourced a runtime that shields a running service while the patch is still in testing

OpenAI and Cisco launched Shirudo on 23 November, an open-source Linux runtime that applies temporary protections to a running service while the permanent patch is still in testing. The problem it addresses is the window between finding a bug and shipping the fix, when an exploit and a legitimate request may rely on the same Linux operation. Shirudo uses eBPF to observe kernel and network activity and to block vulnerable code paths without restarting the system. A team can test whether a proposed shield blocks the exploit while allowing legitimate requests through, then deploy it once an operator approves. In the launch post's controlled test, a Codex-generated shield added an input check to Java's Base64 decoder and attached it to a running Elasticsearch process, blocking a Java4Shell exploit attempt while the service continued running. The code is on GitHub under BSD 2-Clause, and the technical walkthrough is a Linux Plumbers Conference talk.

Why it matters

The gap between disclosure and patch is where exploitation happens, and this is a tool aimed squarely at it. The launch post's test is the vendors' own, on a single named vulnerability, so treat the result as a demonstration rather than a measured protection rate. The open-source licence and the public conference walkthrough are what make this worth a card rather than a vendor claim: a third party can inspect the code and reproduce the walkthrough. Australian and New Zealand operators running long-lived Linux services with slow patch cycles should read the quickstart before relying on it.

DeepMind used proof-checked agent teams to find cheaper quantum algorithms

Google DeepMind published a paper on 23 November describing agent teams of Gemini 4 that improved well-known quantum algorithms, with every result proved in Lean, a formal theorem prover. The teams worked independently, verified each other's ideas in Lean, and met at a virtual conference every four hours to trade and debate them. The reported results include an 11.8× reduction in computing cost for a circuit that calculates a property of inertial confinement fusion, lowering required memory from 5,650 to 4,338 error-corrected qubits, and an 8× cost cut for simulating a common light-sensitive molecule. For Cytochrome P450, an enzyme that breaks down most human medicines, the teams found a 2.5× improvement and lowered memory from 481 to 333 qubits. The Lean library, problem specifications and verified solutions are open-source on GitHub, and the paper is on arXiv.

Why it matters

The method is the story, not the numbers. A quantum circuit too large to simulate classically cannot be tested the usual way, so a proof-checked agent pipeline is a credible way to trust the result. The Lean proofs are machine-checkable, so a third party can verify the work without rerunning the agents. The figures are DeepMind's own, measured on its own problem specifications, but the open-source library makes the claims reproducible in principle. Australian and New Zealand teams running scientific computing on constrained hardware should read the library's problem specifications rather than the headline multipliers.

Three rules that shape what appears

Freshness is a state-change test, not a date test. An item qualifies when something moved — a model shipped, a rule commenced, a vulnerability was exploited, a subpoena was served, a paper published. The window is 48 hours, 72 across a weekend bridge, and it is stated on every edition. Anything older appears in the executive summary as context, never as an entry.

An empty theme stays empty. Where a theme has no qualifying item it is listed in the coverage table with the count it earned and rendered as no section at all — never as a zero bar and never padded with stale material.

Suppression. A story that ran in either of the previous two editions is not repeated unless the day adds a fact, and the new fact must be what the entry is about.
